::: keypoints
- Engagement can happen before a decision, while it’s being made, and after it takes effect.
- The floor: state and local law require notice before meetings, comment during them, and boards and commissions residents can serve on. Some decisions still happen out of public view.
- The ceiling: officials can go further, with options like longer notice, civic assemblies, and oversight committees.
- Having a chance to engage isn’t the same as being able to use it. Schedules, language, distance, internet access, and trust all get in the way.
:::

Engagement can happen before the government makes a decision, while it is being made, and after it takes effect. At each stage, there are important opportunities for residents, corporations, and others outside the government to shape the decisions to meet their interests.

::: figure
{{> phases}}

The three stages of public engagement. Each one has its own page in this section.
:::

::: wide
| Stage | Why | Common examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Before** (notice, education) | Opportunities to learn about the issue and its impacts, so that later engagement is well-informed and impactful | Public notices, mailing lists, staff reports, explainers, educational sessions, city and county email lists, etc. |
| **During** (feedback, deliberation) | Offering feedback on what the proposed decisions do well and poorly, so that they can be improved before they are implemented | Public comment during council meetings, advisory committees, budget hearings |
| **After** (accountability, appeals) | Checking in to make sure that the implemented policy is doing what it was intended to do, with reasonable resources | Regular reporting on results, audits, appeals, oversight bodies, lawsuits, etc. |
:::

## The floor: what is the minimum public engagement that the government has to support?

There are three main ways the public is *required* to be included under state and local law: **public notices** ahead of meetings, **comments** during public meetings, and the many **boards and commissions** that members of the public can serve on. This is what we call the “floor” of public engagement, but public agencies and officials can always choose to do more.

::: wide
| Opportunity | Stage | What the law requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Notices** | Before | Public meetings need advance notice: at least 48 hours for regular meetings and 24 hours for special ones. Property owners near a land use decision must get mailed notice, and state agencies must publish notice at least 21 days before adopting rules. ([ORS 197.763](https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/197.763), [ORS 183.335](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors183.html)) |
| **Public comment** | During | Public meetings that take in-person testimony must also allow remote testimony. Public comment is required at hearings for land use cases, local budget decisions, state rule changes, and utility rate or water right proceedings. Enterprise zone tax agreements longer than three years need resolutions adopted at public meetings. ([Oregon Public Meetings Law](https://www.oregon.gov/transparency/pages/public-meetings.aspx)) |
| **Boards and commissions** | During and/or after | These groups meet regularly to provide public oversight and make decisions about certain parts of government. For example, under [Oregon’s Local Budget Law](https://www.ci.carlton.or.us/media/9666), local governments must have citizen budget committees made up of appointed members of the public and city or county councilors. Planning commissions work in a similar way, reviewing and approving land use proposals (Statewide Planning Goal 1). At the state level, the Governor appoints members to more than 265 state boards and commissions, and electric cooperatives hold member-elected board elections. |
:::

There are also a few notable places where decisions can move out of public view. These practices aren’t necessarily meant to shut the public out. They let the government carry out its work efficiently.

- **Legislative versus executive decisions.** Councils, commissions, and the Legislature set policy in public meetings, where the public can engage more directly. Executive offices and government staff then carry out their duties under those policies, and can make decisions or set priorities with little public review. Public review of every single government decision would quickly grind public service to a halt. Instead, most government activity can be looked into through meeting records, public records requests, and other means.
  - In Prineville, community input on enterprise zone agreements came through elected officials (the mayor, city council, and county commissioners), with no separate public process.
- **By-right zoning.** Many land use decisions go through a planning commission before they are final, but some uses are allowed “outright” or “by right,” meaning they skip that review. This is also meant for efficiency, when a use is considered safe and already fits what the city or county wants to develop. In Prineville, for example, data centers are allowed outright in the industrial park on the rim of the city, where utilities have already been upgraded to support them.
- **Nondisclosure agreements (NDAs).** Larger businesses often sign NDAs that keep the details of their negotiations and business private unless they choose to share them.
  - For data centers in particular, this may be changing. In October 2026, Amazon said it no longer uses NDAs with the government agencies it works with on its projects, following a similar move by Microsoft. This could give the public earlier notice before major projects come to town, though Amazon’s change applies only going forward. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/03/amazon-responds-to-data-center-backlash-says-it-no-longer-uses-ndas/), Oct 3, 2026)

## The ceiling: where could the public have more involvement?

::: figure
{{> floor-ceiling}}

Examples of the floor (what the law requires) and the ceiling (what’s possible) at each stage of a decision.
:::

**The “ceiling” is how much power the public could have, beyond what the law requires.** Depending on funding and interest, public officials can create more opportunities for meaningful public engagement.

**Who gets to take part?** Having a chance to engage is not the same as being able to use it. Meetings may happen during working hours, go on for hours, or follow rules that limit what people can say. Barriers in Central Oregon include language and interpretation, disability access, work and caregiving schedules, distance and transportation, internet access, and trust in institutions. These look different across ages: a working parent, a retiree, and a high school student each face different hurdles. Two questions to keep in mind for every stage: who is missing, and who keeps getting asked?

**What could this look like?** Many of the ideas above the floor draw on the principles of good deliberation:

- **Representative.** The people taking part reflect the whole community, not just those with the time to show up. Civic assemblies do this by randomly selecting participants, and stipends, childcare, and interpretation help more people say yes.
- **Informed.** Participants get time and balanced information to understand an issue before weighing in. Examples include briefing materials, site visits, and Q&A sessions with experts from different sides.
- **Dialogic.** People talk with each other and with decision-makers, not just at them. A facilitated small-group discussion with planning staff allows back-and-forth that a three-minute comment slot doesn’t.
