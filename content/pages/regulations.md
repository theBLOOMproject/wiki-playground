::: keypoints
- Regulations rein in possible harms from private industry: moratoriums, limits on tax breaks, electricity cost rules, and license plate reader rules.
- The situation is moving fast. Gov. Kotek has set a 60-day deadline for the Legislature to pass data center rules.
- Residents shape regulation through advocacy and agenda setting, public hearings, and advisory bodies.
:::

Who’s involved
: City councils, county commissions, and the State Legislature

What rules are already in place
: Public comment processes are used during regulatory decisions

Potential impacts and tradeoffs
: Regulations protect the public interest, but they have to balance effective oversight against rules that are too restrictive, given how uncertain emerging technology is

Where the public could have a say
: Comments at council meetings, advocacy, and proposing community-informed policies

Case study
: [Bend’s license plate cameras](#case-license-plate-cameras)

Regulations help rein in the potential negative effects of private industry, and place guardrails and guidance around the actions of government, corporate, and individual actors to serve the public interest.

**Examples:**

- **Data center moratoriums** (temporary bans) at the local level, passed by city councils. Hillsboro, Salem, and Woodburn have passed them ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/15/woodburn-moratorium-new-data-centers/)). At the state level, the Governor has paused data center permits until July 2027.
- **Limits on data center tax breaks** passed by the Oregon Legislature (HB 4084, 2026).
- **Rules on electricity costs** set by the Oregon PUC under the POWER Act (2025), so that very large users pay for the new power infrastructure they need.
- **License plate reader rules** at the state level, which then shape local police purchasing decisions. Senate Bill 1516, signed in March 2026, added requirements like end-to-end encryption, and a statewide ban on Flock-type cameras has been proposed for 2027 ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/); [Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

Governor Kotek recently announced a 60-day deadline for the state Legislature to pass more informed regulations ([Oregon Capital Chronicle](https://oregoncapitalchronicle.com/2026/11/06/oregon-gov-kotek-says-data-center-rules-universal-preschool-among-priorities-for-next-4-years/)). This situation is evolving quickly, and more news is likely in the coming weeks and months.

**Public engagement in regulation:**

- **Advocacy and agenda setting.** Advocacy from residents is often what motivates new rules. This can include petitions, advocacy groups, and research that gathers evidence of potential harms. It usually runs through more organized, well-resourced organizations that have the time, capacity, and connections to reach public officials.
- **Public hearings** at city council, the Legislature, or through online processes are also good opportunities to weigh in.
- **Public advisory bodies** can be formed to help guide what and how to regulate. Oregon’s Data Center Advisory Committee is a good example. It heard testimony from local leaders (including Prineville’s) and took public comment on its preliminary findings through October 24, 2026. Newer innovations like civic assemblies can also serve as advisory bodies.
