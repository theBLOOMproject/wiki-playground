::: keypoints
- Whether the public gets a say on a project at all is often decided by obscure zoning rules. Changing the approval pathway, from staff approval to public hearings and a board vote, was the lever that guaranteed residents a voice on every new project.
- Timing matters. By the time the rules changed, roughly 250 data centers had been built and dozens more were already in the pipeline. Those pending applications were grandfathered in, and a 2026 attempt to undo that stalled over the threat of developer lawsuits.
- The county had to weigh a real tradeoff in public: data centers pay for a large share of the county budget and have lowered property tax rates, but many residents decided the noise, power lines, and sprawl weren’t worth it.
:::

::: lead
After two decades of courting the industry, the Virginia county with the world’s largest concentration of data centers changed its zoning rules so that every new data center must go through public hearings and a vote by elected officials, rather than a staff-level sign-off.
:::

## Basic information

Loudoun County sits in Northern Virginia, about an hour outside Washington, D.C. Starting when data centers were still a niche industry, the county deliberately recruited them, and the area around Ashburn became known as “Data Center Alley,” which the county describes as the largest concentration of data centers in the world. The payoff was enormous: the local Chamber of Commerce reported that data centers generated about $1.2 billion, or 38% of all county revenue, in fiscal year 2026, and the real property tax rate fell from the highest in Virginia to about 80 cents per $100 over fifteen years.

For years, data centers were a “by-right” use in many of the county’s industrial zones. That meant that if a proposal met the written standards in the zoning ordinance, county staff approved it administratively, with no public hearing and no vote by elected officials. In other areas, data centers needed a “special exception,” which requires staff review, a public hearing and recommendation from the appointed Planning Commission, and a second public hearing and final vote by the elected Board of Supervisors.

As the buildings multiplied and crept closer to neighborhoods, so did the complaints: constant equipment noise, new high-voltage transmission lines and substations, thousands of diesel backup generators, and strain on the regional power grid. Board Chair Phyllis Randall said data centers had become the most frequent complaint she heard. Residents who once accepted the argument about lower taxes were now telling her, “I will pay more. Stop building these.” In 2021, Supervisor Juli Briskman proposed remapping where data centers should be allowed, which grew into the county’s two-phase “Data Center Standards and Locations” initiative.

## What did it change? What was interesting about it?

- **Every decision moved onto the public record.** In March 2025, the Board voted 7–2 to make data centers a special exception use everywhere in the county. This didn’t ban data centers; it changed who decides and where. Every new application now goes through two public hearings and a vote by elected officials.
- **The appointed advisory body and the elected board disagreed.** The Planning Commission narrowly recommended against the change in late 2024, preferring more targeted fixes such as steering data centers toward transmission corridors. The Board went ahead anyway.
- **Grandfathering was the compromise.** In a separate 5–4 vote, the Board let applications filed before its February 2025 public hearing continue under the old rules, as long as they were more than 500 feet from homes. That covered 22 applications. Industry groups and the Chamber supported it; environmental groups and many residents opposed it, and Supervisor Briskman argued it amounted to approving 22 new data centers.
- **Process first, standards second.** Phase 1 settled *who* decides. Phase 2, underway since September 2025 and expected to finish in 2027, is working out *what* the rules should be: siting near homes, substations, on-site power generation and battery storage, noise, and building heights.
- **A pause while the rules are written.** In September 2026, the Board voted 7–1–1 to pause consideration of new data center and substation applications for up to 12 months while Phase 2 is completed. The county attorney advised that an outright moratorium would be illegal under Virginia law, so the pause was structured around the state’s 12-month limit for acting on applications. A formal resolution is scheduled for the Board’s November 17, 2026 meeting.

## What worked

- **A structural guarantee instead of case-by-case fights.** Rather than residents having to discover and organize against each project, the rule change made public hearings automatic for every new data center.
- **Sustained public pressure changed the Board’s stance.** Over a few years, the Board went from the industry’s chief recruiter to its regulator. Advocacy groups like the Piedmont Environmental Council kept the issue visible through every hearing, and the Chair explicitly cited residents’ voices as the reason for the change.
- **Splitting the problem in two.** Settling the process question first let the county act relatively quickly, while the slower, more technical work on standards continues with its own round of public input.

## What didn’t

- **The fix came late.** When by-right approval ended, the county already had about 46 million square feet of data centers built or permitted, and much of the remaining land was already zoned for more. Public input now mostly shapes the margins.
- **Earlier decisions proved hard to undo.** In October 2026, a proposal to revoke the grandfathered status of the remaining legacy projects (about 10.5 million square feet) was tabled for lack of support. County staff warned it could expose the county to $60 million or more in lawsuits and damage its reputation with businesses, and even some supervisors who had opposed grandfathering argued it was unfair to change the rules after developers relied on them.
- **Project-by-project hearings can’t answer the big question.** A special exception hearing is still a yes-or-no vote on one project at a time. The larger questions, like how much data center growth the county wants and what it’s willing to give up in revenue to limit it, are being worked out separately and slowly.
- **The county depends on what it’s trying to rein in.** With more than a third of county revenue coming from data centers, every new restriction carries real budget consequences, a point the business community raised throughout the debate.
