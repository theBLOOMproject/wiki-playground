::: keypoints
- Five layers of government overlap in Central Oregon: state, county, city, special districts, and the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs.
- The state sets the rules counties and cities must follow. The federal government manages much of the region’s land.
- Residents of unincorporated places like Sunriver or Terrebonne have no city council, so the county commission and district boards are their main local voices.
- Federal agencies must consult with Tribes, government to government, before decisions that affect them.
:::

Several layers of government overlap in Central Oregon, and each has its own job. The state sets the rules that counties and cities must follow. The federal government sits above all of them and manages much of the region’s land, but this book focuses on state and local decisions. {src: [Layers of Government in Oregon](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1pQzROp3F_ySr_Dbe2pNVPy9VXi9HEH4GCRJ_6-6LqUg/edit?tab=t.0); [Who Governs Central Oregon](https://claude.ai/artifact/VC2TmuLmLzsX3auubhXxvt)}

::: wide
| Layer | In one sentence | Who is in charge | What it does (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **State** | Sets the rules for everyone in Oregon | Governor, Legislature (30 senators, 60 representatives), state courts, agencies and commissions | Rules for land use, public meetings, and budgets; utility rates; water rights; environmental permits; school funding; state highways; tax incentive programs |
| **County** | Serves everyone in the county and governs rural areas | Elected board of commissioners, plus an elected sheriff, district attorney, clerk, and assessor | For everyone: elections, property taxes, public health, jail. For rural areas: zoning, permits, sheriff patrol, county roads |
| **City** | Runs local services inside city limits | Elected council and mayor; a hired city manager | Zoning and permits, water and sewer, streets, police, enterprise zone tax breaks |
| **Special district** | Handles one specific service | Its own elected board | School districts, community colleges, park, library, fire, and irrigation districts, electric cooperatives |
| **Tribal** | Governs the Warm Springs Reservation as a sovereign nation | Elected Tribal Council of the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs | Makes its own laws on the reservation; works with the state government to government |
:::

**Cities.** Central Oregon has eight cities: Bend, Redmond, Sisters, and La Pine (Deschutes County); Prineville (Crook County); and Madras, Culver, and Metolius (Jefferson County). Every city has an elected council that sets policy and adopts the budget. Many use a professional manager or administrator to run day-to-day work. {verify: for each city} Fire, libraries, and much of parks and recreation come from special districts, and the county sheriff handles policing in some smaller cities.

**Rural, unincorporated areas.** Many people live outside the eight cities, in places like Sunriver, Terrebonne, Tumalo, Crooked River Ranch, and Camp Sherman. These areas are served mainly by the county, plus any special districts that cover them. Residents there have no city council, so their main local voices are the county commission and district boards.

**Regional and education bodies.** Some bodies cover parts of several counties, like the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council (COIC). Public schools, community colleges, and universities also have their own boards and decision-making power, within state and federal law.

**Warm Springs Reservation.** When federal agencies make decisions that affect Tribes, they must consult with them. The [Bureau of Indian Affairs](https://www.bia.gov/service/tribal-consultations) describes Tribal consultation as a formal, two-way, government-to-government conversation held *before* a decision. It calls for at least 30 days’ notice, decision-makers taking part, a record of what was said, and a response explaining how Tribal input was used. For Oregon state agencies, see ORS 182.162 to 182.168.
