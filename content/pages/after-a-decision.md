::: keypoints
- Accountability means reporting results, justifying spending, and closing the loop with the people who gave input.
- Oregon expects to publish a list of state AI uses, and public-facing state AI must offer a way to give feedback.
- Ways to challenge a decision include school board appeals, the Land Use Board of Appeals, public meeting grievances, ballot measures, and lawsuits.
- In land use appeals, an issue usually must be raised at the local hearing first, or it can’t be raised on appeal.
:::

## Accountability

::: lead-strong
**The government shares what happened after a policy takes effect, checks that it was a good use of public money, and is ready to change course if needed.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Accountability means public officials, through open processes, explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, report on results, and take responsibility for how things turned out. At the very least, it means closing the loop with the people who gave input. Kathi Barguil said institutions often lose people at this step: they ask for ideas, then never say how the input was used or why it wasn’t. “Community members want to know that the time that they put in mattered.”

**What it looks like.**

- **Feedback requirements for technologies.** A public list of state AI uses is expected by mid-2027, along with an annual report to the Governor and an AI adoption plan from each agency. Public-facing AI must have a way for users to give feedback, with review and escalation.
- **Addressing concerns.** The Oregon Department of Land Conservation and Development (DLCD) developed an Offshore Wind Energy Roadmap setting standards for offshore wind development. The process involved extensive public review and input from a multi-stakeholder Roundtable assembled in 2024. In July 2026, DLCD published the final Roadmap alongside a detailed document of responses to public comments, showing how community feedback was considered and worked into the final standards.
- **Audits.** The Oregon Secretary of State’s Audits Division reviews how state agencies spend money and whether programs work, and [publishes its findings](https://sos.oregon.gov/audits/Pages/default.aspx).

## Appeal

::: lead-strong
**If a decision or policy isn’t working, or wasn’t made properly, the public should have ways to challenge it.**
:::

**Why it matters.** No one can predict exactly how a policy will turn out once it is in place. Giving the public ways to appeal decisions lets us improve policies so that potential harms can be avoided.

**What it looks like.**

- **School decisions.** In Bend-La Pine, a concern goes first to the school, then to the superintendent’s office, then to the school board, which decides whether an action followed policy.
- **Land use decisions** can be appealed to the [Land Use Board of Appeals (LUBA)](https://www.oregon.gov/luba). One catch: an issue usually must be raised at the local hearing, or it can’t be raised on appeal. This applies only to quasi-judicial and legislative land use decisions, but most land use decisions involving data centers or infrastructure fall into these categories.
- **Public meeting violations.** Anyone can file a written grievance with the public body within 30 days; the body has 21 days to respond. If it doesn’t fix the problem, the person can complain to the [Oregon Government Ethics Commission](https://www.oregon.gov/ogec/Pages/default.aspx). Courts can also void decisions made in an unlawful meeting.
- **Pushing for proper process.** Oregon Housing and Community Services emailed a handbook change barring funded organizations from using AI, “effective immediately.” NeighborImpact argued that enforceable guidance must go through rulemaking with public comment, and the change is now on hold.
- **Ballot measures.** Oregon voters can refer laws and many local decisions to the ballot, or propose their own through an initiative.
- **Lawsuits.** Individuals and advocacy groups can go to court to challenge decisions, enforce public records laws, or block government actions that violate statutory or constitutional rights.
