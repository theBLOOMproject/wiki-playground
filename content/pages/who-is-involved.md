::: keypoints
- Government makes the decision, but residents, organized groups, advocacy groups, and businesses all try to shape it.
- Residents who organize tend to have more influence, because officials see them as speaking for a broader group.
- Business deals are often harder for the public to see. Early recruitment is often confidential, and companies may hire lobbyists.
- Lobbyists who work with the Legislature or state officials must register and file reports anyone can search.
:::

Many groups take part in public decisions, and each plays a different role. Government makes the decision, but residents, advocacy groups, and businesses all try to shape it.

| Who | Their role | Central Oregon examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Government** | Makes and carries out decisions. State decisions often set the rules that local governments must follow. | State agencies, county commissions, city councils, school boards, special districts |
| **Individuals** | Every resident has the right to take part, through the forms of engagement listed above. | Speaking at a council meeting, emailing a commissioner |
| **Organized residents** | When residents organize, officials tend to see them as speaking for a broader group, not just one person. That gives them more influence. | La Pine residents’ response to a proposed data center |
| **Advocacy groups** | Speak for a cause or community. They often follow issues closely and comment on behalf of members. | Central Oregon LandWatch (land use) |
| **Businesses and business groups** | Often have significant sway. They may sell services to government, seek incentives to locate here, or hire lobbyists. | EDCO, data center companies, AI companies such as Anthropic |

**Business decisions are often harder for the public to see.** Many government decisions involve companies that are selling services to the government or working with it to move to the area. Compared to public policy decisions, these are often much less open. Companies may also hire people to meet with officials, build relationships, and influence decisions. This is called lobbying. In Oregon, people who lobby the Legislature or state officials above certain thresholds must register with the [Oregon Government Ethics Commission](https://www.oregon.gov/ogec/pages/lobbying-overview.aspx) and file quarterly spending reports that anyone can [search](https://www.oregon.gov/ogec/public-records/Pages/Public-Records-Search.aspx). {added: OGEC. Verify whether any lobbying rules apply to local governments.}

**The business view, in its own words.** EDCO describes its role as recruiting, keeping, and supporting businesses: “No vote, no permit, no rate authority.” It says early recruitment is often kept confidential to protect companies’ competitive information and workers. It calls these confidentiality agreements a real limit, not bad faith, though it often means the public learns of a project after major terms are set. EDCO says public input comes through elected officials, its three-year community strategy meetings, and public votes on long-term incentives. EDCO’s Jon Stark also said AWS will no longer require communities to sign nondisclosure agreements. {src: EDCO written submission, Oct 13; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14} {gap: link to source requested from Jon Stark}

**What persuades an organization?** Rachel Haakenson (NeighborImpact) listed what tends to work: legal process and past precedent, fit with the organization’s stated values, what partners and funders think, a formal letter to the executive director, and many voices rather than one survey response. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9}
