::: keypoints
- Feedback lets the public weigh in before a policy is final, through comment periods, hearings, surveys, and listening sessions.
- Local examples show feedback changing outcomes, from Bend-La Pine’s classroom AI rules to new transit service in Madras and Bend.
- Comment processes often favor the most motivated voices, and engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change.
- Deliberation, as in a civic assembly, asks residents to weigh tradeoffs together. Doing it well takes time, money, and skilled facilitation.
:::

## Feedback

::: lead-strong
**Members of the public get a chance to weigh in on policies before they are final.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Feedback is a chance for officials to explain what they are considering and why. It also lets the public offer data, personal experience, professional expertise, preferences, and community needs. {src: Utah book}

**What it looks like.** Spaces for public feedback look different for different government bodies.

::: wide
| Who | What they did | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bend-La Pine Schools** | Board policy goes through required public comment. Parent and student voice councils also give input. | The classroom chatbot lost its human-like persona and now flags emotional conversations to teachers. Adults now disclose AI use in grading. Teachers may not use AI plagiarism-detection tools on English-learner students. {verify: against policy text} |
| **NeighborImpact** | For its biennial plan, surveys about problems (not proposed solutions) were distributed to those receiving services, with listening sessions in every city plus online and Spanish-language sessions. (NeighborImpact is not a government entity, but it faces similar challenges, and its experience can help inform government practices.) | Decision-makers hear residents directly, and are more likely to prioritize what they heard. |
| **Cascades East Transit** | Spanish-language survey of Madras residents with the Latino Community Association, under an ODOT grant. | Surveys and fare data led to 15-minute service on Third Street and a planned microtransit pilot in south Bend. |
| **State of Oregon** | The Responsible AI Usage Policy for executive agencies took public comment on a draft in late spring. | Released June 2026. |
:::

{src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14; Karen Rush mtg, Oct 15}

**Challenges.**

- **Who is heard.** Good feedback reflects the whole community, not just a few loud voices or tokenized people. But comment processes often favor the most motivated people, professionals, and interested parties with their own goals and the time, capacity, and training to do it effectively. Creating effective spaces for public consultation takes funding and training many departments don’t have. {src: Utah book}
- **Knowing where to go.** A Tribal delegate said his community wants to know who to talk to about its main concerns, like protecting treaty rights. Formal rules can also limit what counts. In the Tribes’ appeal of Deschutes County’s approval of the Thornburgh resort’s fish and wildlife plan, LUBA found the county did not have to consider the Tribes’ new evidence. {src: Delegate conversation, Oct 21; KWSO; Bend Bulletin; Deschutes County case 247-25-000229-A}
- **What to engage them on.** Some decisions are already fixed by law, so asking the public about them is just box-checking. Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9 via Rachel}

## Deliberation

::: lead-strong
**The public can be invited to discuss and weigh the tradeoffs of a decision together.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Inside the government, deliberation happens all the time: councils and boards debate before they vote. The public is rarely asked to do this, but when it is done well, it can produce well-informed policies that the public supports. Wendy Willis of Oregon’s Kitchen Table put it this way: when people must decide together, “the incentives are problem-solving incentives rather than [political] incentives.” {src: CC Mtg 1, Sep 25}

**What it looks like.** Juries are the most common example of public deliberation in practice, where a randomly selected group of residents learns about an issue and makes recommendations. However, there are many other examples of this, including:

- **Deschutes County** formed a civic assembly on youth homelessness in fall 2025. {src: [Bend City Manager’s Report](https://bendoregon.gov/es/112525-city-managers-report/); Healthy Democracy case study} {team: Unfinished in ORBB: “…that identified …”}
- **Oregon’s Citizens’ Initiative Review** brings together a random panel of voters to study a ballot measure and write a statement for the voters’ pamphlet summarizing their decision and their reasoning.
- **Mosier, Oregon.** After a train derailment, Oregon’s Kitchen Table ran a quick community conversation so the town knew what residents wanted before negotiating with the railroad.

**Challenges.**

- **Doing deliberation well takes a lot of time and money.** Recruitment of a representative group is especially challenging for the reasons above; the people who are most likely to show up are often those with more privilege in one way or another (socioeconomic status, English-speaking, etc.).
- **Lack of training.** Running a good meeting (one that tries to achieve a balance of voices, navigate conflict, etc.) often requires experienced facilitators, who are often hard to find.
