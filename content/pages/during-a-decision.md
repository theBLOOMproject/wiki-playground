::: keypoints
- Feedback lets the public weigh in before a policy is final, through comment periods, hearings, surveys, and advisory councils.
- Local examples show feedback changing outcomes, from Bend-La Pine’s classroom AI rules to new transit service in Madras and Bend.
- Comment processes often favor the most motivated voices, and the same few people get asked again and again.
- Deliberation, as in a civic assembly, asks residents to weigh tradeoffs together. Every method has its own strengths and limits.
:::

## Feedback

::: lead-strong
**Members of the public get a chance to weigh in on policies before they are final.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Feedback is a chance for officials to explain what they are considering and why. It also lets the public offer data, personal experience, professional expertise, preferences, and community needs. {src: Utah book}

**What it looks like.** Public comment periods, hearings, surveys, listening sessions, and advisory councils. Local examples:

| Who | What they did | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bend-La Pine Schools** | Board policy goes through required public comment. Parent and student voice councils also give input. | The classroom chatbot lost its human-like persona and now flags emotional conversations to teachers. Adults now disclose AI use in grading. Teachers may not use AI plagiarism-detection tools on English-learner students. {verify: against policy text} |
| **NeighborImpact** | For its biennial plan: surveys about problems (not proposed solutions), listening sessions in every city plus online and Spanish-language sessions, incentives, trusted liaisons, and tracking who has responded to fill gaps. Board members lead sessions themselves. | Decision-makers hear residents directly, in the room. |
| **COIC / Cascades East Transit** | Spanish-language survey of Madras residents with the Latino Community Association, under an ODOT grant. | Surveys and fare data led to 15-minute service on Third Street and a planned microtransit pilot in south Bend. |
| **State of Oregon** | The Responsible AI Usage Policy for executive agencies took public comment on a draft in late spring. | Released June 2026. |

{src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14; Karen Rush mtg, Oct 15}

**Challenges.**

- **Who is heard.** Good feedback reflects the whole community, not just a few loud voices. But comment processes often favor the most motivated people, professionals, and advocacy groups with their own goals. {src: Utah book} Doing this well takes funding and training many departments don’t have.
- **The same few people.** Kathi Barguil said the same few people of color are often asked to speak for their communities, especially in election season. Karen Rush (Bend-La Pine) said leaning on the same engaged people causes burnout, student councils lean toward academically driven students, and in-person meetings in downtown Bend limit people from La Pine and other outlying areas. {src: CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14; Karen Rush mtg, Oct 15}
- **Knowing where to go.** A Tribal delegate said his community wants to know who to talk to about its main concerns, like protecting treaty rights. Formal rules can also limit what counts. In the Tribes’ appeal of Deschutes County’s approval of the Thornburgh resort’s fish and wildlife plan, LUBA found the county did not have to consider the Tribes’ new evidence. {src: Delegate conversation, Oct 21; KWSO; Bend Bulletin; Deschutes County case 247-25-000229-A}

## Deliberation

::: lead-strong
**Sometimes the public is invited to discuss and weigh the tradeoffs of a decision together.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Inside government, deliberation happens all the time: councils and boards debate before they vote. The public is rarely asked to do this, but when it is done well, it can produce well-informed policies that the public supports. Wendy Willis put it this way: when people must decide together, “the incentives are problem-solving incentives rather than rhetorical incentives.” Taha Elwefati added that meeting face to face keeps an issue from becoming something flat to yell at online. {src: CC Mtg 1, Sep 25}

**What it looks like.** Juries, advisory bodies, task forces, and civic assemblies, where a randomly selected group of residents learns about an issue and makes recommendations. Examples:

- **Deschutes County** formed a civic assembly on youth homelessness in fall 2025. {added: [Bend City Manager’s Report](https://bendoregon.gov/es/112525-city-managers-report/), Nov 2025}
- **Oregon’s Citizens’ Initiative Review** brings together a random panel of voters to study a ballot measure and write a statement for the voters’ pamphlet. {added: verify current status}
- **Mosier, Oregon.** After a train derailment, Oregon’s Kitchen Table ran a quick community conversation so the town knew what residents wanted before negotiating with the railroad. {src: Mtg 2 team debrief, internal}
- **Cambridge, Massachusetts** convened a civic assembly within months of a dispute over gunshot-detection microphones. {src: Mtg 2 team debrief, internal} {verify: }

**Comparing common methods.** This table compares methods without ranking them; judging them is left to delegates. {src: Research Doc} {added: summary drafted from general knowledge; review}

::: wide
| Method | Strengths | Limits | Who usually takes part | Cost and time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Public hearing** | Open to all; on the official record | Short time slots; little back-and-forth | Most motivated residents, organized groups | Low |
| **Survey** | Reaches many people quickly | Answers are shallow; wording shapes results | Depends on outreach; often online users | Low to medium |
| **Listening session** | Two-way conversation; flexible | Not always on the record; may not reach everyone | Those invited or nearby | Medium |
| **Advisory council** | Ongoing input; members build expertise | Small group; can become insiders | Appointed or volunteer members | Medium |
| **Community meeting** | Informal; builds relationships | Results can be hard to capture | Neighbors, local groups | Low to medium |
| **Civic assembly** | Random selection mirrors the community; time to learn and weigh tradeoffs | Costly; recommendations usually not binding | Randomly selected residents | High |
:::
