::: keypoints
- Feedback lets the public weigh in before a policy is final, through comment periods, hearings, surveys, and listening sessions.
- Local examples show feedback changing outcomes, from Bend-La Pine’s classroom AI rules to new transit service in Madras and Bend.
- Comment processes often favor the most motivated voices, and engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change.
- Intermediary groups like NeighborImpact and Oregon’s Kitchen Table often have more room than government to reach people.
- Deliberation, as in a civic assembly, asks residents to weigh tradeoffs together. Doing it well takes time, money, and skilled facilitation.
:::

## Feedback

::: lead-strong
**Members of the public get a chance to weigh in on policies before they are final.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Feedback is a chance for members of the public to share why a proposed policy or plan will or will not work for their community. They can offer data, personal experience, professional expertise, preferences, and community needs, to help plans meet the needs and values of more people. It is also a chance for public officials to explain what they are considering and why.

**What it looks like.** Spaces for public feedback look different for different government bodies. Below are a few examples from formal and informal public bodies.

::: wide
| Who | What they did | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bend-La Pine Schools** | Board policy goes through required public comment. Parent and student voice councils also give input. | The classroom chatbot lost its human-like persona and now flags emotional conversations to teachers. Adults now disclose AI use in grading. Teachers may not use AI plagiarism-detection tools on English-learner students. |
| **NeighborImpact** | For its biennial plan, surveys about problems (not proposed solutions) were distributed to those receiving services, with listening sessions in every city plus online and Spanish-language sessions. (NeighborImpact is not a government entity, but it faces similar challenges, and its experience can help inform government practices.) | Decision-makers hear residents directly, and are more likely to prioritize what they heard. |
| **Cascades East Transit** | Spanish-language survey of Madras residents with the Latino Community Association, under an ODOT grant. | Surveys and fare data led to 15-minute service on Third Street and a planned microtransit pilot in south Bend. |
:::

Intermediary organizations like NeighborImpact, COCAP, and Oregon’s Kitchen Table help connect the community with decision-makers. They often have more room to maneuver than government: engagement is their main focus, they have the training, and they don’t have to work within the same rules and priorities that can hamstring government agencies.

**Challenges.**

- **Who is heard.** Good feedback reflects the whole community, not just a few loud voices or tokenized people. But comment processes often favor the most motivated people, professionals, and interested parties with their own goals and the time, capacity, and training to do it effectively. Creating effective spaces for public consultation takes funding and training many departments don’t have, or haven’t set aside for that purpose.
- **What to engage them on.** Some decisions are already fixed by law, so asking the public about them is just box-checking. Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change.

## Deliberation

::: lead-strong
**The public can be invited to discuss and weigh the tradeoffs of a decision together.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Making good decisions requires people not only to gather good information, but to talk through the values they hold and the tradeoffs between different options. Inside a government body, deliberation happens all the time: councils and boards debate before they vote. The public is rarely asked to do this to inform policies, but when it is done well, it can produce decisions that most residents support. Wendy Willis of Oregon’s Kitchen Table put it this way: when people must decide together, “the incentives are problem-solving incentives rather than [political] incentives.”

**What it looks like.** The many boards and commissions across Central Oregon have elements of deliberation: residents who aren’t elected officials discuss public issues together and make decisions. But these conversations involve only a small group of people, not the wider public. And when the public is brought in for things like public comment, there is rarely room for two-way conversation between residents or with their representatives.

The gold standard is deliberation that is **inclusive, representative, informed, and conversational**: many different kinds of affected people talking and considering each other’s reasons for what they believe. Juries are the most common example of public deliberation in practice, where a randomly selected group of residents learns about an issue and makes recommendations together. But there are many other examples:

- **Deschutes County** formed a civic assembly on youth homelessness in fall 2025 that identified 22 actionable recommendations.
- **Oregon’s Citizens’ Initiative Review** brings together a random panel of voters to study a ballot measure and write a statement for the voters’ pamphlet summarizing their support (or lack of it) and their reasoning.
- **Mosier, Oregon.** After a train derailment, Oregon’s Kitchen Table ran a quick community conversation so the town knew what residents wanted before negotiating with the railroad.

**Challenges.**

- **Doing deliberation well takes a lot of time and money.** Recruitment of a representative group is especially challenging for the reasons above; the people who are most likely to show up are often those with more privilege in one way or another (socioeconomic status, English-speaking, etc.). Overcoming this takes a lot of time and money, which can quickly exhaust both participants and government staff.
- **Lack of training.** Running a good meeting (one that tries to achieve a balance of voices, navigate conflict, etc.) often requires experienced facilitators, who can be hard to find and expensive to hire.
