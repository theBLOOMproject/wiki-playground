::: keypoints
- Notice: people need to hear about decisions early enough to contribute. Oregon law requires meeting notices and mailed notices for many land use hearings, but renters and people farther away can be missed.
- Education: accurate, balanced information helps people push for what helps and against real harms. Committee members disagreed about how much opposition comes from misunderstanding.
- Due diligence: decision-makers need both the facts and a sense of what the public wants.
- Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change.
:::

## Notice

::: lead-strong
**People need to hear about decisions that matter to them early enough to contribute.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Simply put, you can’t weigh in on something you don’t know about. When people are left out, government can make decisions that accidentally harm them, and trust drops, which makes compromise harder later. Local leaders said people are often brought in only after a decision is mostly made. Rachel Haakenson (NeighborImpact) noted that much is decided before feedback is invited, and that engagement after a plan exists becomes endorsement. Kathi Barguil (Mixte Communications) said engagement should start at the very beginning, with the community helping define the problem and who is affected. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14}

**What it looks like.**

- **Meeting notices.** Oregon’s Public Meetings Law requires notice of every meeting of a governing body: at least 48 hours ahead for regular meetings and 24 hours for special ones. Notices must list the date, time, place, how to join online, and the main topics. {added: [City of Springfield summary of OGEC training](https://springfield-or.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Memo-Oregon-Public-Meeting-Law_Final.pdf), Dec 2025}
- **Mailing lists.** Many local governments let you sign up to get public notices and agendas by email, including Crook County and the City of Prineville.
- **Land use notices.** Before many land use hearings, cities and counties must mail notice at least 20 days ahead to nearby property owners: within 100 feet inside an urban growth boundary, 250 feet outside one, and 750 feet in farm or forest zones. Renters and people farther away may not get a letter. {added: [ORS 197.763](https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/197.763)}

## Education

::: lead-strong
**People need accurate, balanced information to take part in a meaningful way.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Well-informed people can push for what will truly help them and against real harms. This includes learning about the topic itself (for example, data centers), what decision is being made, who makes it, and on what timeline. Several speakers raised misinformation. Teresa Coats said people need a basic understanding of AI before deciding, since views shaped by the news, friends, or a layoff can lead to snap decisions. Adam Young pointed to a local claim that Ochoco Reservoir is low because of data centers as a reason for myth-versus-fact resources. Others pushed back on this framing: Robin Hayakawa questioned the idea that opposition comes from not understanding AI, and Taha Elwefati said the knowledge gap runs both ways. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14}

**What it looks like.**

- Policy explainers, local news and civic media, and sit-downs with experts
- Media literacy
- Learning from places that have faced similar problems
- Asking specific questions. Instead of only asking “data center or no data center,” Adam Young suggested asking where it would go and what its effects would be on the environment, light, and noise. {src: Member input v.3, Oct 7}
- Balanced education sessions *before* public comment, which Taha Elwefati suggested may be a missing step delegates could recommend. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9}

**Challenges.** Good information often has to be pulled from many sources. Robin Hayakawa noted it is hard to expect people to dig into heavy, technical issues “unless it was their job to understand them,” which leaves residents relying on elected and appointed officials. {src: Member input v.3, Oct 7}

## Due diligence

::: lead-strong
**Decision-makers need good information from all sides, and they need to weigh the possible effects on the community.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Public officials are expected to explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, and report on results. Knowing they will be expected to do so reinforces the importance of careful decision-making and stewardship of public funds, as well as responsiveness to the people they serve. {src: Utah book}

**What it looks like.** Officials often ask for reports, briefings, and expert advice, and staff help produce them and connect officials with experts. This work has two parts: *the facts* (what is actually happening?) and *public preferences* (what do people actually want?). Locally, the City of Prineville says it treats data centers like any large industrial project: study the demand, check whether there is enough capacity, then negotiate development agreements and incentives. {src: CC Mtg 2, Oct 1}

**Challenges.** Some decisions are already fixed by law, so asking the public about them is just box-checking. Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9} {verify: Rachel Haakenson or Taha Elwefati}
