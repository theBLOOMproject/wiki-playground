::: keypoints
- Notice: people need to hear about decisions early enough to contribute. Oregon law requires meeting notices and mailed notices for many land use hearings, but renters and people farther away can be missed.
- Education: accurate, balanced information helps people push for what helps and against real harms. Misinformation can get in the way.
- Due diligence: decision-makers need both the facts and a sense of what the public wants, ideally gathered in the open.
:::

## Notice

::: lead-strong
**People need to hear about decisions that matter to them early enough to contribute.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Simply put, you can’t weigh in on something you don’t know about. When people are left out, the government can make decisions that accidentally harm them, and trust drops, which makes compromise harder later. Local leaders said people are often brought in only after a decision is mostly made.

Rachel Haakenson (NeighborImpact) noted that much is decided before feedback is invited, and that engagement after a plan exists becomes endorsement. Kathi Barguil (Mixte Communications) said engagement should start at the very beginning, with the community helping define the problem and who is affected. {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14}

**What it looks like.**

- **Meeting notices.** Oregon’s Public Meetings Law requires notice of every meeting of a governing body: at least 48 hours ahead for regular meetings and 24 hours for special ones. Notices must list the date, time, place, how to join online, and the main topics. {src: [City of Springfield summary of OGEC training](https://springfield-or.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Memo-Oregon-Public-Meeting-Law_Final.pdf), Dec 2025}
- **Mailing lists.** Many local governments let you sign up to get public notices and agendas by email (e.g., Crook County and the City of Prineville).
- **Land use notices.** By law, cities and counties must mail notice at least 20 days ahead to nearby property owners for many land use decisions: within 100 feet inside an urban growth boundary, 250 feet outside one, and 750 feet in farm or forest zones. Renters and people farther away may not get a letter. ([ORS 197.763](https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/197.763))

## Education

::: lead-strong
**People need accurate, balanced information to take part in a meaningful way.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Well-informed people can push for what will truly help them and against real harms. This includes learning about the topic itself (for example, the positive and negative impacts of using AI in the classroom), what decision is being made, who makes it, and on what timeline.

On the other hand, misinformation can be a significant barrier to productive engagement. Multiple public officials in the region noted that they had seen misinformation about the impacts of AI-powered technologies, data centers, and more influence how people reacted to proposals. Adam Young pointed to a local claim that Ochoco Reservoir is low because of data centers as a reason for myth-versus-fact resources.

**What it looks like.**

- Accessible, balanced educational materials that explain the nature of the decisions being made, pros and cons, and more
- Opportunities to sit down with public officials who are responsible for the decision
- Funding local news and information distributors
- Media literacy {team: Unfinished in ORBB: “Media literacy for …”}
- Learning from places that have faced similar problems
- Asking specific questions. Instead of only asking “data center or no data center,” Adam Young suggested asking where it would go and what its effects would be on the environment, light, and noise. {src: Member input v.3, Oct 7}

**Challenges.** Good information often has to be pulled from many sources, and gathering it can be very time intensive. People may not already agree on which details are important, or even what the truth of a matter is.

Robin Hayakawa (of Central Oregon LandWatch) also noted it is hard to expect people to dig into heavy, technical issues “unless it was their job to understand them,” which leaves residents relying on elected and appointed officials. {src: Member input v.3, Oct 7} In other places (such as the Hanford Advisory Board on nuclear waste cleanup), public commissions of everyday residents have taken many months or even years to understand the impacts.

## Due diligence

::: lead-strong
**Decision-makers need good information from all sides, and they need to weigh the possible effects on the community.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Public officials are expected to explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, and report on results. Knowing they will be expected to do so reinforces the importance of careful decision-making and stewardship of public funds, as well as responsiveness to the people they serve. {src: Utah book}

**What it looks like.** Officials often ask for reports, briefings, and expert advice, and staff help produce them and connect officials with experts. This work has two parts: *the facts* (what is actually happening?) and *public preferences* (what do people actually want?). Ideally, this information gathering is transparent to the public, so that residents and advocates can call out potential gaps or draw their own conclusions.

Locally, the City of Prineville says it treats data centers like any large industrial project: study the demand, check whether there is enough capacity, then negotiate development agreements and incentives. {src: CC Mtg 2, Oct 1}
