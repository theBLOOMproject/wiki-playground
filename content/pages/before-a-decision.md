::: keypoints
- Advance notice: people need to hear about decisions early enough to contribute. Oregon law requires meeting notices and mailed notices for many land use hearings, but renters, rural residents, and people farther away can be missed.
- Information: accurate, balanced information helps people push for what helps and against real harms. Misinformation can get in the way, and officials can counter it by sharing accurate data early.
- Due diligence: decision-makers need both the facts and a sense of what the public wants, ideally gathered in the open.
:::

## Advance notice

::: lead-strong
**People need to hear about decisions that matter to them early enough to contribute.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Simply put, you can’t weigh in on something you don’t know about. When people are left out, the government can make decisions that accidentally harm them, and trust drops, which makes compromise harder later. Local leaders said people are often brought in only after a decision is mostly made.

> “I think it’s important to have the community engagement piece far enough in advance from the organizational standpoint, the decision making standpoint, that decisions aren’t already made. It has to be still in the point where you can adjust strategies and not even really change the plan.”
>
> Rachel Haakenson, NeighborImpact

**What it looks like.**

- **Meeting notices.** Oregon’s Public Meetings Law requires notice of every meeting of a governing body: at least 48 hours ahead for regular meetings and 24 hours for special ones. Notices must list the date, time, place, how to join online, and the main topics.
- **Mailing lists.** Many local governments let you sign up to get public notices and agendas by email (e.g., Crook County and the City of Prineville).
- **Land use notices.** By law, cities and counties must mail notice at least 20 days ahead to nearby property owners for many land use decisions and public hearings: within 100 feet inside an urban growth boundary, 250 feet outside one, and 750 feet in farm or forest zones. Renters and people farther away may not get a letter. ([ORS 197.763](https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/197.763))

**Challenges.** “Neighbor notice” of specific land use applications goes only to landowners within a few hundred feet of the proposed project. In rural areas, that can amount to very few people.

Neighbor notices can also be hard to understand, because they rely on legal and land use jargon. State and local rules require notice of certain public hearings to be posted in the newspaper, but newspaper circulation is falling, and few Central Oregonians rely on the classified section of the local paper for news about local government.

## Information

::: lead-strong
**People need accurate, balanced information to take part in a meaningful way.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Well-informed people can advocate for what will truly help them and against real harms. This includes learning about the topic itself (for example, the positive and negative impacts of using AI in the classroom), what decision is being made, who makes it, and on what timeline.

On the other hand, misinformation can be a significant barrier to productive engagement. Multiple public officials in the region noted that they had seen misinformation about the impacts of AI-powered technologies, data centers, and more influence how people reacted to proposals. While they are not the source of misinformation, local governments and elected officials can counter it by proactively reaching out to residents and making sure accurate, transparent data is widely available.

> “Central Oregon has massive disparities in AI literacy. We need to help get everyone up to speed on the risks and opportunities AI poses to our communities.”
>
> Open Poll participant. 67% of participants agreed.

> “There needs to be more education on AI for the general public before many of these statements can be addressed. …no way to know if what I am reading/seeing is real or AI so I don’t trust it anymore.”
>
> Open Poll participant. 85% agreed.

Adam Young pointed to a local claim that Ochoco Reservoir is low because of data centers as a reason for myth-versus-fact resources.

**What it looks like.**

- **Accessible, balanced educational materials** that explain the nature of the decisions being made, pros and cons, and more
- **Opportunities to sit down with public officials** who are responsible for the decision
- **Funding local news** and information distributors
- **Asking specific questions.** Instead of only asking “data center or no data center,” Adam Young suggested asking where it would go and what its effects would be on the environment, light, and noise.
- **Learning from places that have faced similar problems**

**Challenges.** Good information often has to be pulled from many sources, and gathering it can be time intensive. People may not already agree on which details are important, or even what the truth of a matter is.

Depending on the decision and any confidentiality limits, there may not be enough time or information for the general public to learn the nuances and the potential pros and cons of an issue. Passionate advocacy based on an incomplete or incorrect understanding can lead to worse decisions. It can also break down trust on both sides, which can color discussions well into the future.

It is generally hard to expect people to dig into heavy, technical issues unless it is their job to understand them. This leaves residents relying on elected and appointed officials. In other places, public commissions of everyday residents have taken many months or even years to understand the impacts.

## Due diligence

::: lead-strong
**Decision-makers need good information from all sides, and they need to weigh the possible effects on the community.**
:::

**Why it matters.** Public officials are expected to explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, and report on results. Knowing they will be expected to do so reinforces the importance of careful decision-making and stewardship of public funds, as well as responsiveness to the people they serve.

**What it looks like.** Officials often ask for reports, briefings, and expert advice, and staff help produce them and connect officials with experts. This work has two parts: *the facts* (what is actually happening?) and *public preferences* (what do people actually want?). Ideally, this information gathering is transparent to the public, so that residents and advocates can call out potential gaps or draw their own conclusions.

As a local example, the City of Prineville’s planning department says it treats data centers like any large industrial project: study the demand, check whether there is enough capacity, then negotiate development agreements and incentives.
