## Who decides what in Central Oregon

*See also: [Why “public engagement”?](#goal-of-engagement)*

- **Land and natural resources:** Most land in the region is public, almost all of it federal: more than 77% of Deschutes County and more than half of Crook County ([USDA NRCS, Deschutes](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/state-offices/oregon/deschutes-county); [Crook](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/state-offices/oregon/crook-county)). It is managed by the [Deschutes National Forest](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r06/deschutes), [Ochoco National Forest](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r06/ochoco), and [BLM Prineville District](https://www.blm.gov/office/prineville-district-office). The [Oregon Department of Forestry](https://www.oregon.gov/odf/aboutodf/pages/mapoffices.aspx) runs its Central Oregon District from Prineville.
- **Zoning and building:** Counties handle zoning and permits outside city limits (for example, [Deschutes County Community Development](https://www.deschutescounty.gov/640/Zoning-101) and [Crook County Planning](https://crookcountyor.gov/1307/Planning-Department)). Cities handle them inside city limits.
- **Traffic and roads:** [ODOT Region 4](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/regions/pages/index.aspx) runs state highways. County road departments run county roads ([Deschutes](https://www.deschutescounty.gov/259/Road), [Crook](https://crookcountyor.gov/1250/Road-Department)). Each city runs its own streets.
- **Schools:** Each school district has its own elected board: Bend-La Pine, Redmond, Sisters, Crook County, Jefferson County, Culver, Black Butte, and Ashwood. The [Oregon Department of Education](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/about-us/Documents/CombinedDirectory_20260922_034305.pdf) sets statewide standards.
- **Public health:** Each county runs a health department ([Deschutes](https://www.deschutescounty.gov/265/Health-Services), [Crook](https://crookcountyor.gov/1260/Health-Department), [Jefferson](https://www.jeffersoncountyor.gov/publichealth/page/community-services)), overseen by the [Oregon Health Authority](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/localhealthdepartmentresources/pages/lhd.aspx).
- **Economy:** Cities and counties handle planning, permits, and incentives like enterprise zones. The state works in the region through the Governor’s [Central Oregon Regional Solutions team](https://www.oregon.gov/gov/regional-solutions/Pages/Central.aspx). The nonprofit [Economic Development for Central Oregon (EDCO)](https://www.edcoinfo.com/about-edco) recruits and supports businesses.

Decisions about artificial intelligence are no exception. They land in many of these same places: school boards set AI use policies, cities and counties approve data center sites and tax incentives, and state agencies set rules for their own use of AI. *(See [What AI-related decisions are happening in Central Oregon?](#range-of-decisions))*

## Who gets involved: more detail

*See also: [Who gets involved in public decisions?](#who-is-involved)*

**Organized residents, a local example.** La Pine residents’ response to a proposed data center.

**What works?** Rachel Haakenson (NeighborImpact) listed what tends to work: legal process and past precedent, fit with the organization’s stated values, what partners and funders think, a formal letter to the executive director, and many voices rather than one survey response.

**Lobbying.** Companies may also hire people to meet with officials, build relationships, and influence decisions. In Oregon, people who lobby the Legislature or state officials above certain thresholds must register with the [Oregon Government Ethics Commission](https://www.oregon.gov/ogec/pages/lobbying-overview.aspx) and file quarterly spending reports that anyone can [search](https://www.oregon.gov/ogec/public-records/Pages/Public-Records-Search.aspx).

## The floor: more legal detail

*See also: [The floor](#when-to-get-involved)*

In Oregon, the public has a right to watch most government decisions being made, but usually not a right to speak, except in certain cases like land use and budget hearings. Governing bodies set their own rules for public comment.

- **State rules.** If 10 people (or a group with 10 members) ask, a state agency must hold a hearing on a proposed rule. Anyone can sign up for an agency’s rule notices.
- **Utilities and water.** Residents can comment in Oregon Public Utility Commission cases and on water right applications with the Oregon Water Resources Department. The 2025 POWER Act created a separate rate class so large data centers pay their own grid costs, but it covers only investor-owned utilities (Pacific Power, PGE, Idaho Power). Co-ops like Central Electric Cooperative answer to member-elected boards instead.
- **Business tax incentives.** For enterprise zone agreements, the company must file before breaking ground, and denials can be appealed to the Oregon Tax Court. The Strategic Investment Program is a 15-year tax break with an annual community service fee paid to local taxing districts.
- **Enterprise zones in Prineville.** The City said the climate around public input on enterprise zone agreements could be different today.

**Land use timing.** How much residents can affect a project depends on when they get involved.

| Stage | What is happening | What residents can still change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Setting the rules** | A city or county adopts or changes its comprehensive plan or zoning | The most: what kinds of projects are allowed, and where |
| **Discretionary permit** | A project needs a hearing to show it meets set criteria | Whether it meets those criteria, and what conditions are attached |
| **Allowed outright** | The project is already allowed under existing zoning (“by right”) | Very little; staff may approve it without a hearing (as with data centers in Prineville’s industrial park) |

**Don’t forget the simplest options.** Anyone can call or email an elected official, or talk to one at the grocery store.

The takeaway: the landscape is complex. Which rules apply depends on who is deciding, what kind of decision it is, and how far along it is.

## Before, during, and after: more examples

*See also: [Before a decision](#before-a-decision), [During a decision](#during-a-decision)*

- **Starting early.** Kathi Barguil (Mixte Communications) said engagement should start at the very beginning, with the community helping define the problem and who is affected.
- **Learning takes time.** The Hanford Advisory Board on nuclear waste cleanup is one example of a public commission of everyday residents that took a long time to understand the impacts.
- **State of Oregon feedback.** The Responsible AI Usage Policy for executive agencies took public comment on a draft in late spring, and was released in June 2026.
- **Knowing where to go.** A Tribal delegate said his community wants to know who to talk to about its main concerns, like protecting treaty rights. Formal rules can also limit what counts. In the Tribes’ appeal of Deschutes County’s approval of the Thornburgh resort’s fish and wildlife plan, LUBA found the county did not have to consider the Tribes’ new evidence.
