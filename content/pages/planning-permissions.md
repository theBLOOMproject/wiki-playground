::: keypoints
- Planning decides whether, where, and on what terms a community wants new development like data centers.
- Planning departments often engage residents while shaping long-range plans; there are usually smaller chances once plans are being finalized.
- Deals with large companies, like community benefit agreements and tax incentives, are usually negotiated privately, often under nondisclosure agreements.
:::

In the local context, this is most relevant to data centers and other physical AI investments. The goal of these decisions is to figure out *whether, where, and on what terms* a community wants new development.

**Planning** sets the long-term vision for a community’s economy and land, as well as policies that might encourage (or discourage) certain kinds of economic growth. This may include things like:

- **Comprehensive (general) plans** set the overall vision and rules for land use in a city or county. These are often developed by the Planning Department of the city or county.
- **Economic Development Strategy (EDS):** A regional economic plan developed by extragovernmental entities, like the Economic Development Corporation of Central Oregon or the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council ([COIC](https://www.coic.org/ceds/)). {team: Unfinished in ORBB: “These plans typically include key areas of investment, …”}
- **Economic Opportunities Analysis (EOA):** Oregon’s land use rules require cities to plan for enough land and economic opportunities to support their expected growth over the next 20 years. Economic Development for Central Oregon (EDCO) helps local governments develop these analyses.
- **Enterprise development zones,** where special tax breaks, regulatory relief, and other incentives might make it easier for larger corporations to bring labor, tax revenue, and more to the region.
- **Urban Growth Boundary (UGB) adjustments** decide where a city can grow. In Oregon, a UGB expansion needs approval from both the city and the county.

**Before these plans are established:** It is common practice for planning departments to do thorough community engagement when shaping up larger planning documents and strategies. This helps to align the plans for the city or county with the community’s vision for where they live, their values, the risks that they see, and more. The extent of engagement varies based on the entity involved and the type of planning document that they are producing:

- EDCO engages residents in the region when it refreshes its Economic Development Strategy every three years. {team: ORBB placeholder: “This involves …”}
- The city of Arcata, CA engaged its residents in a lengthy community engagement process when creating its economic development plan ([Arcata](https://lostcoastoutpost.com/loco-media/loco-media/agendizer/attachment/26288/1.pdf)).
- It is common for cities and counties to engage their residents in years-long engagement processes to develop their general plans, which include things like zoning decisions (which kinds of businesses are allowed to be where), investment priorities, transportation planning, and more.

**As the plans are actually developed, there are usually smaller opportunities for the public to weigh in.** In some cases, committees or advisory boards might be established to help provide real-time feedback as the details of the planning documents are established. At the very least, most of these documents are officially adopted by the local governments, who may hold public meetings to discuss the details of the plans before they are officially voted on. In some cases, residents have organized petitions against certain aspects of a plan (e.g., urban growth boundaries in Prineville), which have motivated changes in the scope or details of planning decisions.

While the public may have some opportunities for feedback in the earlier stages, decisions with larger corporations and developers are more likely to depend on private negotiations between the city or county and that private entity. These decisions might include things like:

- **Community Benefit Agreements (CBAs)** are basically a way of saying, “You can come here on these conditions, to make sure you help our community thrive.” This might include everything from the corporation paying for essential infrastructure upgrades to providing grants for public schools, hiring locals for projects, and more. They are negotiated between the city or county and the corporation, and are made official through a contract. Some processes for determining community benefit agreements are more inclusive of the community than others.
- **Incentives:** In order to bring Meta and Apple to Prineville, the city’s leaders waived property taxes for these corporations for 15 years.

**These discussions are usually much more private.** Companies will often establish nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) with the city to protect their strategic plans, financials, and other information from giving their competitors a leg up; this also prevents the public from knowing many details about these plans before they are established. Recently, companies like Amazon have ended their practice of establishing NDAs after widespread public outcry. {check: Needs a source.} A key challenge is to figure out what information the public needs to know (and when) in order to guide decisions in their best interest.
