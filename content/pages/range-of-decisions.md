::: keypoints
- Research found about 29 AI-related decisions by public bodies affecting the three counties between late 2023 and October 2026. About half involved data centers.
- From government’s perspective, these boil down to three types: government use, permissions and planning, and regulations.
- Public input on government purchases is uncommon and often comes after the fact, though contract renewals offer another chance.
- Planning decides whether, where, and on what terms projects like data centers can locate. Regulation reins in possible harms.
:::

One of the main places where Central Oregonians can have a say in how AI shows up in their lives is through **state and local government decisions.** Our research found about 29 AI-related decisions made by public bodies affecting Deschutes, Crook, and Jefferson counties between late 2023 and October 2026. About half involved data centers. Others involved policing, schools, government operations, and elections ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

## Where AI decisions are made

::: wide
| Kind of decision | Central Oregon example | Who decides | How residents can take part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buying AI tools for government | Bend’s Flock license plate cameras; Bend’s use of Copilot; Bend-La Pine’s MagicSchool | City manager or police chief (smaller purchases); city council (larger contracts); school district | Public comment at council meetings; public records requests; pushing for review when contracts come up for renewal |
| Rules for how government uses AI | City of Bend AI policy (2025); Deschutes County AI policy (2024); Bend-La Pine Schools AI policy (2025) | City manager (administrative policy); school board | School board public comment periods. City and county staff policies usually have no formal public input |
| Economic planning | Prineville’s Economic Opportunities Analysis (2026); COIC’s regional economic strategy | Planning commission and city council; COIC | Planning commission hearings; comments on draft plans |
| Land use and growth boundaries | Prineville’s proposed industrial expansion; about 200 acres of industrial land added near Madras | City and county together | Joint public hearings; appeals (the Madras decision was appealed by Central Oregon LandWatch and the Jefferson County Farm Bureau) |
| Selling or siting land for a project | La Pine’s proposed land sale for a 20 MW data center | City council | Public comment; showing up at meetings (La Pine’s May 2026 meetings were packed) |
| Tax breaks | Meta’s and Apple’s enterprise zone deals in Prineville; the 2026 state pause on new data center tax breaks | City and county (zone sponsors); state legislature | Council and county meetings; testimony to legislators |
| Power costs | New electricity rate rules for very large users like data centers | Oregon Public Utility Commission (PUC) | Written public comment; consumer groups can formally join (“intervene” in) a case |
| Environmental permits | Air and water permits for data centers (paused statewide until July 2027) | Oregon Department of Environmental Quality, at the Governor’s direction | Public comment periods on individual permits |
| Statewide rules | Senate Bill 1516 (2026) on license plate readers; expected 2027 AI bills; the Data Center Advisory Committee | Legislature; Governor | Testimony at hearings; contacting legislators; comments to the advisory committee |
| What happens in classrooms | Bend-La Pine’s 2026 review of all classroom technology, including AI | School board | Petitions; public comment at board meetings |

## From the government’s perspective, it boils down to three types of decisions

::: wide
| Type | What government is deciding | Key documents | Where the public usually fits in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Government use** | Whether and how government itself uses AI tools | Requests for qualifications, contracts, AI use policies | Mostly after the fact, unless the purchase is large or controversial |
| **2. Permissions and planning** | Whether and where private AI investments (like data centers) can locate, and on what terms | Economic plans, comprehensive plans, growth boundaries, permits, tax agreements | Hearings on long-range plans and specific projects |
| **3. Regulations** | How to rein in possible harms from private industry | Laws, ordinances, administrative rules, utility orders | Agenda setting, advisory bodies, public hearings |
:::

## 1. Government use (procurement and AI use policies)

As described above, government agencies are already using AI tools, mostly to help staff work faster. “The rationale for the public sector to use these systems is similar to private sector: increase efficiency and speed of transactions and lower the costs” (Hodjick 2022). {check: This citation may be Hickok (2022), “Public procurement of artificial intelligence systems: new risks and future proofing,” *AI & Society*. Confirm author and add a link.}

**Procurement** is the process government uses to buy goods and services.

| Tool | Who bought it | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microsoft Copilot | City of Bend | Only AI chatbot approved for city staff |
| Flock Safety license plate cameras | Bend Police Department | Bought with grant funds; contract not renewed after public pushback |
| MagicSchool AI | Bend-La Pine Schools | Procurement guided by state (ODE) guidelines |

Key points:

- Larger purchases are usually defined through a **Request for Qualifications (RFQ)** or similar request, which describes what the government needs.
- They are settled through a **contract** with the company providing the services.

**Public engagement in procurement:**

- **Transparency is generally required to preserve trust and accountability with the public.** Secrecy in public contracting can lead to waste, corruption, and loss of public trust ([OGP, The Costs of Secrecy](https://www.opengovpartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/OGP_Costs-of-Secrecy_20180515.pdf)). Bid documents are typically posted for the public on portals like [oregonbids.com](http://oregonbids.com) (see the [City of Bend example](https://www.oregonbids.com/government-agencies/deschutes/bend-city-of-41286/)).
- **Small purchases may skip public review entirely.** For small dollar amounts, staff can buy things under their own purchasing authority. This was the case with the Flock cameras. In Bend, the city manager can add up to $250,000 to a contract the council already approved without going back to the council ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)). {new: }
- **Some experts argue procurement rules should require more from AI companies:** “Procurement regulations should be updated to include obligations for the developers to share details of data qualities, model decision decisions, optimization techniques and processes when required by the public entity. Additionally, procurement guidelines should require a capable internal workforce to be in place before a procurement decision is made for an algorithmic system.” {check: This quote has no citation in the outline; it likely comes from the same Hickok (2022) article.}

However, public comment, voting, and other public input *before and during* purchasing decisions is generally uncommon, for some good reasons:

- **Keeping the market fair.** Governments don’t want to give one supplier an unfair advantage, or force a private company to disclose information that would put it at a disadvantage compared to its competitors.
- **Workload.** Opening every purchase to public input would overwhelm staff, taking time away from other important work.
- **Technical expertise.** Many details of the services being bought require technical expertise that most residents (and many officials) don’t have.

On the other hand, governments can create **oversight bodies** to watch over certain kinds of purchases. Examples include public policing commissions, or Bend’s Human Rights and Equity Commission, which received updates on the Flock decision ([City of Bend HREC minutes, Jan 13, 2026](https://bendoregon.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/HREC-1.13.2026-Meeting-Minutes-Draft_Lisa-Larsen.pdf)). {new: }

**After purchasing decisions are made,** the public can still have influence. Contracts come up for renewal, and councils can review them. In Bend, public pressure led the city not to renew the Flock contract, and later to promise a council vote and public input before adding new license plate cameras from Axon ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)).

**AI use policies** set the rules for how employees, teachers, or students may use AI.

- **In education,** many guidelines come down from higher levels of government. For example, the Oregon Department of Education created thorough guidelines for districts ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). Districts then write their own policies (see the [Bend-La Pine case study](#case-bend-la-pine)).
- **In state government,** agencies follow a statewide Responsible AI Usage Policy and an AI risk management framework. Public-facing AI tools must post a disclosure card ([State uses, from Amy Paul](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1va1NYHXVXybVj7PxIA7K6IRd_El7Lhiz/view?usp=drive_link)).
- **In local government,** policies like Bend’s are usually set by the city manager as administrative rules. The Bulletin’s editorial board said the public had no chance to weigh in on Bend’s policy ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {check: Find and link the Bulletin editorial.}

## 2. Permissions and planning

In the local context, this is most relevant to data centers and other physical AI investments. The goal of these decisions is to figure out *whether, where, and on what terms* a community wants new development.

**Planning** sets the long-term vision for a community’s economy and land.

- **Comprehensive Economic Development Strategy (CEDS):** A regional economic plan developed by the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council ([COIC](https://www.coic.org/ceds/)).
- **Economic Opportunities Analysis (EOA):** Oregon’s land use rules require cities to plan for enough land and economic opportunities to support their expected growth over the next 20 years. Economic Development for Central Oregon (EDCO) helps local governments develop these analyses. This is where economic opportunities like data centers are often mentioned. Prineville’s 2026 EOA found the city was out of large (50+ acre) industrial sites, with data centers a key driver, and estimated it needs about 755 acres ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {check: The outline says EOAs are required “every 20 years.” They plan for a 20-year land supply, but cities update them more often; confirm wording.}
- **Comprehensive (general) plans** set the overall vision and rules for land use in a city or county.
- **Urban Growth Boundary (UGB) adjustments** decide where a city can grow. In Oregon, a UGB expansion needs approval from both the city and the county.

**Public engagement in planning:** Good engagement here focuses on the *vision* of the community, not the deep technical details. The extent of engagement depends on the specific place and department. Different places have different practices:

- **Prineville, OR:** Economic planning is led mostly by EDCO, consultants, and city staff. EDCO does not do much proactive community engagement (it’s not really their job), but the city holds public reviews and planning commission hearings.
- **Arcata, CA (for comparison):** Arcata developed its plan through a series of co-design workshops and information sessions, in addition to consultant input ([Arcata](https://lostcoastoutpost.com/loco-media/loco-media/agendizer/attachment/26288/1.pdf)).

**Community Benefit Agreements (CBAs)** are basically a way of saying, “You can come here on these conditions, to make sure you help our community thrive.”

- They are negotiated between the city (or community groups) and the corporation.
- They are made official through a contract.
- They can involve the community more directly in deciding what a project must give back.

Central Oregon does not appear to have a formal CBA for a data center yet. Related examples include Meta’s community grants program, which Crook County helps promote ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)), and Meta’s reported $5 million+ in contributions to local schools and nonprofits ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). Prineville leaders have also told the state they will not offer full tax breaks to future data centers ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {team: The outline asks for CBA examples that involved the community more thoroughly. EDCO suggested looking at the consumer-owned utility model in Umatilla and Morrow counties.}

**Permitting** is the step where government approves (or denies) a specific project. For a data center, this can include:

- Land use and building permits from the city or county.
- Air and water pollution permits from the Oregon Department of Environmental Quality (currently paused for data centers until July 2027) ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/10/13/oregon-data-centers-technology-amazon-tina-kotek/)).
- Water rights from the Oregon Water Resources Department.
- Agreements with the electric utility to connect to the grid.

## 3. Regulations

Regulations rein in the potential negative effects of private industry.

**Examples:**

- **Data center moratoriums** (temporary bans) at the local level, passed by city councils. Hillsboro, Salem, and Woodburn have passed them ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/15/woodburn-moratorium-new-data-centers/)). At the state level, the Governor has paused data center permits until July 2027.
- **Limits on data center tax breaks** passed by the Oregon Legislature (HB 4084, 2026).
- **Rules on electricity costs** set by the Oregon PUC under the POWER Act (2025), so that very large users pay for the new power infrastructure they need.
- **License plate reader rules** at the state level, which then shape local police purchasing decisions. Senate Bill 1516, signed in March 2026, added requirements like end-to-end encryption, and a statewide ban on Flock-type cameras has been proposed for 2027 ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/); [Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

**Key documents:** Laws (statutes), local ordinances, administrative rules, and utility commission orders.

**Public engagement in regulation:**

- **Agenda setting.** Advocacy from residents is often what motivates new rules. This can include petitions, advocacy groups, and research that gathers evidence of potential harms.
- **Advisory bodies** can be formed to help guide what and how to regulate. Oregon’s Data Center Advisory Committee is a good example. It heard testimony from local leaders (including Prineville’s) and took public comment on its preliminary findings through October 24, 2026 {src: EDCO written submission, Oct 13}.
- **Public hearings** at city council, the legislature, or through online processes are also good opportunities to weigh in.
- **Utility commission cases** allow consumer groups (like the Citizens’ Utility Board) and others to formally participate in decisions about who pays for new power infrastructure.
