::: keypoints
- The Commission’s power comes from timing. Under a 2018 ordinance, any city department that wants a surveillance technology must bring it to the Commission, with a written impact report and use policy, *before* acquiring it, and report on its use every year after.
- Notable wins include a warrant requirement for police cell-phone tracking devices and the end of a little-known agreement between the police and federal immigration enforcement.
- Still, the Council doesn’t always listen to the Privacy Commission’s recommendations, and a volunteer board with no staff of its own struggles to get complete information from the departments it oversees.
:::

::: lead
When residents learned the city planned to turn a port security system into a citywide surveillance hub, a coalition of residents, community groups, and privacy advocates pushed the city to scale it back, and won a standing citizen commission that reviews every surveillance technology the city wants to use before the City Council decides.
:::

## Basic information

In July 2013, Oakland accepted a federal Homeland Security grant to build a “Domain Awareness Center” (DAC). Originally meant to monitor the Port of Oakland, the plan grew into a citywide hub that would pull together feeds from roughly 700 cameras (including at schools and public housing), license plate readers, gunshot detectors, and facial recognition software. This came just as Edward Snowden’s leaks about federal surveillance were making headlines.

A coalition of residents, community groups like Oakland Privacy, and the ACLU of Northern California pushed back through lobbying, packed council meetings, and street protests. Brian Hofer, who had never set foot in City Hall before, became one of the leading voices. In March 2014, the City Council scaled the DAC back to the port and airport and created an ad hoc citizens’ committee, with members appointed by each councilmember, to write a privacy and data retention policy before the scaled-back center could switch on. It took about a year, partly because there were so few models to borrow from anywhere in the country.

The ad hoc committee recommended making itself permanent. In January 2016, the Council created the nine-member Privacy Advisory Commission (PAC), which began meeting that July. The Commission’s first major task was drafting a citywide surveillance law with city staff, which the Council adopted in May 2018.

## What did it change? What was interesting about it?

- **Review happens up front, not after the fact.** Any city department that wants to acquire surveillance technology must first go before the Commission with a Surveillance Impact Report and a proposed Use Policy, even if the technology costs nothing. The Commission recommends; the Council decides.
- **Review continues after approval.** Departments must submit annual reports on how they use existing surveillance tools, so the Commission can keep weighing costs and benefits over time rather than giving a one-time yes or no.
- **Citizens wrote the rules themselves.** The ad hoc committee wrote the DAC’s privacy policy, and the permanent Commission drafted the surveillance ordinance that defines its own role.
- **Enforcement through the courts.** The ordinance lets any resident sue the city for violating it, which gave the Commission’s work teeth that its advisory status alone does not.
- **Not anti-technology by default.** The Commission has backed some tools, unanimously supporting continued use of ShotSpotter gunshot detection and, in 2023, recommending a policy for about 300 new license plate reader cameras. Its job is to weigh tradeoffs, not just say no.

## What worked

- **Surfacing information that would otherwise stay internal.** At the Commission’s second meeting in 2016, a police deputy chief publicly explained for the first time how the department used cell-site simulators, devices that mimic cell towers to locate phones, which it had been using since 2006. The Commission drafted a policy requiring warrants and yearly reports, and the Council adopted it.
- **Investigations led to real policy changes.** The Commission’s look into police assistance during a 2017 ICE raid in West Oakland uncovered an agreement with ICE that the Council later terminated.
- **A concrete fight built a lasting constituency.** Because the Commission grew out of the DAC campaign, it had organized community support from day one, and groups like Oakland Privacy kept showing up to watchdog its work. It became a national model that other cities have studied.

## What didn’t

- **Advisory only, and the Council can override it.** In 2021, after police failed to produce required audits and admitted keeping license plate data for two years instead of the six months its policy allowed, the Commission recommended suspending the program. A Council committee instead just asked the department to hand over the documents. In 2025, the Commission voted 4–2 against renewing the city’s contract with Flock Safety over concerns about license plate data being shared with federal agencies. A Council committee deadlocked on it, but the full Council approved the contract 7–1 in December 2025 with added privacy amendments. Two commissioners had resigned the month before, frustrated that their warnings kept being overridden.
- **It depends on departments sharing information.** The police department repeatedly failed to hand over records the Commission requested. Its chair was told to file public records requests like any member of the public, and eventually sued the city in 2021 as a private citizen to get them.
- **Volunteers with no staff burn out.** Commissioners have day jobs and no attorney, investigator, or dedicated staff, so they mostly rely on what city departments tell them. Members described fatigue and difficulty trusting they had the full picture, and Hofer later concluded the model can’t work without an inspector general to dig into the data on the commission’s behalf.
- **Heavy reliance on one champion.** Commissioners worried openly about what would happen when Hofer left. He stepped down in 2025, around the same time the Council overrode the Commission on the Flock contract.
