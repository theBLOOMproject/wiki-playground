::: keypoints
- Prineville welcomed data centers during an economic crisis, with little public debate. Their tax breaks begin expiring in 2027.
- Crook County’s unemployment fell from nearly 20% to about 6%, but residents are now divided about the data centers.
- La Pine faced a proposal during a time of strong public skepticism. Residents organized quickly, and the council rejected the sale unanimously.
- Both decisions were made by city councils, but the public’s role was very different.
:::

Neither of these cases involves a large “AI data center” of the newest kind. Prineville’s existing data centers were built mostly for cloud computing, and La Pine’s proposal was mainly for cryptocurrency mining. However, they are good examples of how communities make decisions about data centers, and they show two very different outcomes. {team: Content committee advice: present Prineville in small pieces tied to what delegates must decide, so it informs without becoming advocacy. A useful question to pose: *“If not data centers, what?”* Also note that data centers don’t explain all of Crook County’s changes {src: Mtg 2 team debrief, internal}.}

## Prineville

The decision
: Whether to welcome large data centers, and on what terms (tax breaks, land, water, infrastructure)

Who had authority
: City of Prineville and Crook County (land use and enterprise zone tax deals), with help from EDCO

The public’s role
: Council and county meetings where the deals were approved. Little proactive community engagement

Competing views
: Many credit the data centers with saving the local economy. Others say they raised the cost of living, changed the small-town feel, and use too much land, water, and power

What happened afterward
: Tax breaks begin expiring in 2027. The city now says it wants more from future deals

**Timeline:**

::: timeline
1. **2002** An Economic Opportunities Assessment laid out several options for industrial development. {check: Confirm the year and document.}
2. **2008 to 2010** The housing crisis and Great Recession brought the city to its knees. The timber industry was dying, and county unemployment approached 20% ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).
3. **Late 2009 to 2010** A then-secret “well-funded, well-known company” negotiated to build in Prineville’s enterprise zone ([Data Center Knowledge](https://datacenterknowledge.com/business/company-x-plans-oregon-data-center)). Facebook finalized the deal and broke ground in 2010. {new: }
4. **2011 onward** Facebook’s first data center opened in 2011. Apple followed. Both received 15-year property tax breaks through Oregon’s rural enterprise zone program. Each new building got its own deal ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).
5. **2026** Prineville’s new Economic Opportunities Analysis lists data centers as a possible future investment, citing past benefits. City leaders told the state they favor future data centers but will not offer more full tax breaks ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).
6. **2027 to 2035** The data centers’ tax breaks expire one building at a time. Meta’s tax bill is expected to jump from about $178,000 to $4 million in the first year, more than a quarter of the county’s property tax receipts ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).
:::

In the city’s own words, Prineville was “dying on the vine” and didn’t have other options ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). The city pursued a number of economic arrangements to make sure the data centers would benefit the community, including franchise fees on electricity (about $1 million per month today) and company-funded water storage and wastewater projects ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). {check: EDCO and the Mtg 4 minutes give different job and franchise fee figures (350+ vs. 450+ jobs; about $430,000 vs. $400,000 in 2011 franchise fees). Use EDCO’s sourced figures with attribution.}

**Despite the apparent success, there was not much public engagement:**

- The work was led mainly by the city’s economic development department, EDCO, and consultants.
- There was little public pushback at the time. This highlights how today feels different from earlier waves of investment, when data centers served uses people accepted more readily (cloud storage, video streaming, recommendation algorithms).
- Today, residents are more divided. Some credit the data centers for good jobs. Others say the facilities raised the cost of living, light up the night sky, and should be shared with other communities rather than concentrated in one place ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).

**Now, the city says it is in a position to demand more from AI companies,** including and beyond Meta ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/prineville-wants-more-out-of-its-next-data-center-deal/)).

- The 2026 economic plan includes data centers as a potential investment, citing the benefits they brought before. It found the city is out of large industrial sites and needs about 755 more acres ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).
- **The biggest fight in recent planning wasn’t about data centers.** It was about expanding the urban growth boundary for a biomass power plant on what had been farmland. That plan was also developed mostly through private consultants, and it drew public outcry. Most city and county officials pointed out that the city’s growth has depended on steady, strategic boundary adjustments. Many residents argued that converting farmland goes against their community’s values. The city later resumed its plan without the biomass project ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).
- Similar boundary adjustments would be needed to bring in more data centers.
- Content committee members noted that Prineville may be unusual: its industrial land sits on a rimrock away from homes, and it stores water underground through aquifer storage and recovery {src: CC Mtg 2, Oct 1}.

## La Pine

The decision
: Whether to sell about 20 acres in the La Pine Industrial Park to Boxminer.io for a 20-megawatt data center

Who had authority
: La Pine City Council. Because Deschutes County owns the land, the county commission would also have needed to approve the sale

The public’s role
: Public comment at council meetings; petitions

Competing views
: The developer promised jobs and no excessive water use or higher power bills. Residents worried about the power grid, water, noise, and community effects

What happened afterward
: The council rejected the sale unanimously on May 27, 2026

- **Before:** In March 2026, the La Pine City Council voted to start the process of selling the land and asked city staff to learn more about the proposal ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/la-pine-rejects-proposed-data-center-amid-public-outcry/)). The local economic development board recommended the project. Some residents were upset they hadn’t been notified about the March vote ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/after-pushback-to-la-pine-data-center-community-developers-await-next-steps/)). {new: }
- **During:** Nearly 100 residents showed up to a May 13 council meeting, even though the data center wasn’t on the agenda ([KTVZ](https://ktvz.com/news/2026/05/13/proposed-data-center-in-la-pine-creates-crowd-at-meeting/)). {new: } The council heard nearly five hours of public comment that month, mostly against the project. Residents worried about the fragile rural power grid. The 20-megawatt facility would use about 15 times the electricity used by all the households in a city La Pine’s size ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/05/28/la-pine-rejects-data-center-central-oregon/)). {new: } The developer promised “up to” 200 jobs, but the city manager’s report estimated it might be closer to five ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/la-pine-rejects-proposed-data-center-amid-public-outcry/)).
- **After:** The council voted unanimously to reject the sale on May 27, 2026. “We do listen to you because we live here as well,” the council president told the crowd ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/la-pine-rejects-proposed-data-center-amid-public-outcry/)). Some reports describe a citizen ballot measure on future data center projects at the site for the November 2026 election. {check: This is unverified; confirm with the Deschutes County Clerk.}

**Comparing the two:** Prineville welcomed data centers at a time of economic crisis, with little public debate. La Pine faced a proposal during a time of strong public skepticism, and residents organized quickly. Both decisions were made by city councils, but the public’s role was very different.

**Other cases EDCO suggests:** communities that declined projects (La Pine, Christmas Valley, Jefferson County); the consumer-owned utility model in Umatilla and Morrow counties; The Dalles’ dispute over disclosing water use (transparency); and Prineville’s Water Use Fact Sheet and aquifer storage (public-facing data) {src: EDCO written submission, Oct 13}.
