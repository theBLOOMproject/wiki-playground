::: keypoints
- Communities nationwide are pushing back on data centers, and a 2026 poll found two-thirds of Oregon voters support a temporary halt on new AI data centers.
- In October 2026, Gov. Kotek paused new pollution permits for data centers until July 2027, and several Oregon cities have passed their own temporary bans.
- AI investment is enormous, but economists disagree about how much it’s actually driving growth.
- The benefits and costs don’t always fall on the same people, from Prineville’s tax breaks to power bills.
:::

AI has moved from a topic for tech companies to a question for communities. In 2026, it has become a political issue, an economic issue, and an environmental issue, both nationally and here in Oregon.

## Politically

**Many communities are pushing back against data centers.** A national tracker, Data Center Watch, found that local opposition blocked or delayed at least 75 data center projects worth about $130 billion in just the first three months of 2026. That roughly matched the total for all of 2025. The number of active opposition groups more than doubled, and they now span 49 states. More than 300 state bills about data centers were filed in the first six weeks of 2026 ([Data Center Watch](https://datacenterwatch.org/q1-2026)). {new: }

**Oregon voters are skeptical too.** A July 2026 poll of 600 Oregon voters by DHM Research found that about 60% have negative feelings toward AI. Sixty-six percent supported a temporary halt on building new AI data centers, including a majority of both Democrats (77%) and Republicans (51%) ([KLCC/OPB](https://www.klcc.org/politics-government/2026-08-06/oregonians-are-extremely-skeptical-of-data-centers-new-polling-suggests)). {new: }

**State and local leaders are responding.** Data centers became a major issue in the 2026 governor’s race, and both major candidates said they favor a moratorium. In October 2026, Gov. Kotek directed state regulators to pause air and water pollution permits for new or expanding data centers until July 2027. This builds on an earlier pause on data centers on state-owned land ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/10/13/oregon-data-centers-technology-amazon-tina-kotek/)). {new: } Hillsboro, Salem, and Woodburn have passed their own temporary bans ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/15/woodburn-moratorium-new-data-centers/)). {new: } {check: Content committee notes from Oct 9 say “no state ban has been decided.” The governor’s Oct 13 permit pause came after that meeting, so this section should be updated with the committee.}

**People want transparency about government AI use.** In a Central Oregon Civic Action Project poll, the vast majority of respondents agreed that governments should disclose when and how they are using AI and justify the cost to taxpayers ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)).

**Some people worry about bigger, long-term risks.** Some researchers and AI company leaders have warned that very advanced AI could become hard to control. It is also unclear whether AI could simply be “shut off” if something went wrong, since it runs across thousands of servers run by different companies in different countries. Experts disagree about how serious or how close these risks are (adapted from the Utah Briefing Book, “Does AI Have an Off Switch?”).

## Economically

**AI spending is propping up the economy, but economists disagree on how much.** Harvard economist Jason Furman estimated that investment in data centers and information-processing technology made up about 92% of U.S. economic growth in the first half of 2025 ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2025/10/07/data-centers-gdp-growth-zero-first-half-2025-jason-furman-harvard-economist/)). Goldman Sachs economists disagree. They argue that because so many chips and parts are imported, AI’s real boost to U.S. growth was “basically zero” ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/ai-added-basically-zero-to-us-economic-growth-last-year-goldman-sachs-says-2000799940)). {new: } (both)

**There is a lot of promise, but also a lot of speculation.** Major economic institutions project that AI could raise productivity if widely adopted (IMF, 2024; McKinsey Global Institute, 2023; Goldman Sachs, 2023, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book). At the same time, the amount of money being spent is enormous: one estimate puts AI investment in data centers, power, networks, and chips at $10.3 trillion from 2025 to 2032 ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/financing-the-ai-buildout/)). If the expected returns don’t show up, communities could be left with half-used buildings and power lines that someone still has to pay for.

**Who gets the benefits, and who carries the risks?** The answer is not always the same group of people. For example:

- **Taxes:** Without tax breaks, Meta’s and Apple’s Prineville data centers would have owed about $31 million in property taxes last year. With them, the two companies paid about $347,000 combined ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). Statewide, data centers paid $60.2 million in property taxes in 2025, while exemptions were worth an estimated $230 million to $450 million ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/17/oregon-data-centers-consume-quarter-state-power-report/)).
- **Jobs:** Data centers create many temporary construction jobs, but relatively few permanent ones. Of Oregon’s 2,630 direct data center workers, most live in Eastern Oregon ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/17/oregon-data-centers-consume-quarter-state-power-report/)).
- **Power bills:** When utilities build new power plants and lines for data centers, someone has to pay. Oregon’s POWER Act (2025) is meant to keep those costs from shifting onto regular households ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

**What more investment and integration could mean:** As AI is built into more products and services, it gets harder for any one community to say “no” to it. Local governments may feel they need to keep up so they don’t get “left behind” (see [What makes AI-related decisions different](#what-makes-ai-different)).

## Other concerns

**Privacy.** AI makes it easier to collect, combine, and search large amounts of personal data. In Bend, a user error left a national search setting turned on in the police department’s Flock camera system. As a result, federal immigration officials searched Bend’s license plate data 279 times in June 2025, in violation of Oregon’s sanctuary laws ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)). {new: } {check: The Source describes this as a “user error” by Bend PD; confirm wording before publishing. Parents have also raised privacy worries about AI voice recognition in state testing {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}.}

**Environment.** Data centers need large amounts of land, energy infrastructure, and long-term water supplies, all in one place. Central Oregon LandWatch has told the state’s Data Center Advisory Committee that its main concerns are the combined effects on water, energy, land, and communities, and pressure to convert farm and forest land to industrial use ([Central Oregon LandWatch, presentation to ODOE, April 2026](https://www.oregon.gov/energy/get-involved/Documents/2026-04-24-7-Ben-Gordon-Central-Oregon-LandWatch-Data-Centers-Deck.pdf)). {new: } Statewide, information about how much water data centers use is often incomplete or unavailable ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/17/oregon-data-centers-consume-quarter-state-power-report/)). In Prineville, some residents worry about power and water use in a region prone to drought and wildfire ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).

## What’s the next wave?

Several decisions are coming soon that will shape how AI shows up in Central Oregon:

- **The 2027 Oregon legislative session** (starting January 2027). Gov. Kotek plans an AI infrastructure bill, and Amy Paul (Oregon’s Chief AI Officer’s office) expects many AI bills {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14}. A proposal to ban Flock-type license plate cameras statewide is also expected ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).
- **The state Data Center Advisory Committee’s recommendations,** due in October 2026, and the end of the state permit pause in July 2027.
- **Public-facing government AI.** Deschutes County is working toward a chatbot for its website, and Bend is looking for “low risk, high value” uses ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)).
- **AI “agents”** that take actions on their own may start showing up in workplaces and government services.
