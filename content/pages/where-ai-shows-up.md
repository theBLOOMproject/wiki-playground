AI is already part of daily life in Central Oregon, sometimes in ways that we know about, and sometimes in ways that are harder to see. It shows up in local businesses, in the region’s data centers, in classrooms, in policing, and inside government offices.

## Business and workplaces

Many businesses are already using AI for their operations, like customer service, scheduling, marketing, and bookkeeping. Over time, this will likely extend into companies that people depend on every day, such as health care providers, banks, and utilities.

How employers bring AI into the workplace varies. Content committee member Teresa Coats said most employers she works with want employees involved in the process and want to calm fears about job loss, though some are eliminating roles {src: CC Mtg 2, Oct 1}. {team: Teresa’s list of employees’ top 10 concerns about AI is still pending and would fit well here.}

## Data centers

Data centers are not new; they have been the backbone of the internet for decades. They do three main jobs:

1. **Store and retrieve data (“cloud computing”).** This is the most familiar use of data centers, and what the current AI industry is built on. Even before the AI industry took off, data centers were needed to store the data for websites, stream videos on sites like YouTube, and back up photos and email. Over the past few decades, more and more of our information has been stored in data centers.
2. **Run calculations for everyday apps (“inference”).** Data centers have always done the computing behind everyday apps. When you search for something on Google, the algorithms that power the search run on a computer in a data center. Each time someone asks an AI chatbot a question, a data center does that work too.
3. **Train AI models.** This is the newest use for data centers. Training a large model can take thousands of specialized chips running nonstop for weeks or months.

**At the state level,** a first-of-its-kind report from ECOnorthwest and the University of Virginia found the following ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/17/oregon-data-centers-consume-quarter-state-power-report/)):

| Measure | Oregon today |
| --- | --- |
| Operating data centers | 111 |
| Planned or under construction | 32 more |
| Share of state’s retail electricity sales (2025) | About 23% |
| Projected share by 2030 | 31 to 32% |
| Direct data center workers | 2,630 (about 0.2% of the state workforce; 2,205 in Eastern Oregon) |

The state has recently put new rules in place to manage this growth. These include a pause on new enterprise-zone tax breaks for data centers (HB 4084, 2026), a separate electricity rate class for very large power users like data centers (the POWER Act, HB 3546, 2025), and a statewide Data Center Advisory Committee ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {check: Confirm the HB 4084 bill number and whether its pause applies to Prineville’s Long-Term Rural Enterprise Zone.}

**Prineville’s** large data centers are among the first in Oregon, having broken ground with Meta in 2010 (Apple followed soon after). Meta’s Prineville campus has grown to 11 buildings covering 3.2 million square feet, and together the two companies have invested about $8 billion ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). Notably, these were *not* being used for the training or deployment of artificial intelligence, but the size, capacity, and economic significance of these data centers is very comparable to recent data center deals in the AI boom.

These data centers have supported massive improvements to the local economy. In 2010, Crook County’s unemployment rate was close to 20%. Today it is about 6%, and the county had the highest median private-sector wage in Oregon in 2024 ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). The city’s revenue is also projected to rise further as the companies’ property tax breaks begin to expire in 2027 (see the [Prineville case study](#case-data-centers)).

On the other hand, **La Pine** recently faced a heated debate over a proposal for a 20-megawatt data center to be used for AI and cryptocurrency (bitcoin) mining. After hours of public comment, the La Pine City Council rejected the project unanimously in May 2026 ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

## Education

AI is being introduced into classrooms to support teachers, students, and administrators. For example, **in Bend-La Pine Schools** the district has procured MagicSchool AI, a suite of tools to help teachers develop lesson plans and presentations, grade student homework, and more. Students also have access to a chatbot that can help them work through problems or explore their curiosities.

Oregon was one of the first states to give school districts guidance on AI, through the Oregon Department of Education (ODE). Most of this guidance is about how to keep AI use ethical and responsible. That includes protecting student privacy, giving teachers discretion over AI use in their classrooms, and avoiding plagiarism detectors, which have been shown to be biased against multilingual students ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). According to content committee discussions, ODE’s guidance shifted over three versions, moving from encouraging AI use toward leaving decisions to local districts {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9}.

In response, many other school districts and institutions have been putting AI use policies in place before procuring new technologies.

- **Bend-La Pine Schools:** The district adopted an AI policy and an “AI Acceptable Use Scale” in 2025 ([Bend-La Pine Schools](https://www.blschools.org/teaching-and-learning/educational-technology/generative-ai)).
- **In Crook County,** the school board adopted an AI policy in March 2026 and blocked student access to generative AI until the policy was in place ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {check: This comes from an AI-generated meeting summary; verify against official minutes.}
- **At Central Oregon Community College (COCC),** the college created an AI Committee in 2025 to recommend AI policies, review tools, and advise on AI-related cheating ([COCC](https://cocc.edu/policies/general-policy-manual/general/committee-structure/artificial-intelligence-committee)).

## Policing and surveillance

AI-powered cameras can help police departments collect data on people, cars, and other activity in public spaces. **Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs)** have been the most recent subject of debate at the city and regional level. These cameras scan and catalog license plate numbers, along with a car’s make, model, and color.

- **Bend:** The Bend Police Department used grant funds to buy four stationary ALPR cameras from Flock Safety, a fast-growing company that uses AI to log data on all passing vehicles. The cameras sat at two Highway 97 intersections for about seven months as part of a pilot program. After packed city council meetings in early January 2026, the city decided to turn the cameras off and not renew the contract ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/01/08/bend-flock-cameras-ai-license-plate-camera-law-enforcement/)). Community advocacy from residents (a Change.org petition and testimony at council meetings) and local organizations (notably the Latino Community Association) drove the change. A key concern was whether federal immigration officials could access the data.
- **What happened next:** Bend began looking at a new vendor, Axon, for stationary ALPR cameras. After public pressure, the mayor committed in May 2026 to bring that decision to a council vote with public input ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)).
- **Deschutes County:** The county approved a $2.4 million contract with Axon for body cameras, dash cameras, and Tasers, but chose not to turn on the ALPR features ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/deschutes-county-sheriffs-office-to-contract-with-axon-for-body-dash-cams-and-tasers/)). {check: Year of this contract is inferred; confirm against the county commission agenda.}

## Government

Governments are using AI mostly behind the scenes, to help staff work faster ([State uses, from Amy Paul](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1va1NYHXVXybVj7PxIA7K6IRd_El7Lhiz/view?usp=drive_link)):

- **Help with day-to-day tasks:** Microsoft Copilot is approved for all state employees to assist with summarizing emails, tracking follow-ups, research, and brainstorming.
- **AI features inside business software:** Tools like Adobe, Gallup, and Articulate are approved on a case-by-case basis to create training videos and captions, as well as to summarize survey data.
- **Custom-built AI tools:** Solutions built on generative AI models are approved case-by-case after a risk review, such as a chatbot designed to answer simple questions about new Medicaid work rules so caseworkers can focus on more complex inquiries.

Public-facing AI tools used by state agencies must post a public “disclosure card” explaining what the tool does ([ODHS](https://www.oregon.gov/odhs/agency/Pages/ai-disclosure.aspx)).

**Locally,** governments are using AI chatbots to help staff draft and summarize emails, comb through documents, create content for press releases, and rewrite website language ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)):

- **City of Bend:** Staff use is limited to Microsoft 365 Copilot. About 400 of the city’s 800 employees have asked for it. Bend adopted an AI policy in 2025, along with a steering committee and staff training.
- **Deschutes County:** Staff use two chatbots, mostly for research and data analysis, under an AI policy adopted in 2024.

Local governments are not yet using AI tools that the public interacts with directly. Deschutes County is working toward a website chatbot, and Bend says it will look for “low risk, high value” uses first ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)). A content committee member also noted that the city is still looking for opportunities to use AI for public-facing purposes.

The content committee agreed on a scope line for the assembly: community input on AI tools that government uses *to serve the public* is in scope. Internal staff productivity tools (like drafting email with Copilot) are out of scope {src: CC Mtg 1, Sep 25}.
