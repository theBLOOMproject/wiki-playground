AI is already part of daily life in Central Oregon, often in ways people don’t see. It shows up in local businesses, in the region’s data centers, in classrooms, in policing, and inside government offices.

## Business and workplaces

Many businesses are already using AI for their operations, like customer service, scheduling, marketing, and bookkeeping. Over time, this will likely extend into companies that people depend on every day, such as health care providers, banks, and utilities.

How employers bring AI into the workplace varies. Content committee member Teresa Coats said most employers she works with want employees involved in the process and want to calm fears about job loss, though some are eliminating roles {src: CC Mtg 2, Oct 1}. {team: Teresa’s list of employees’ top 10 concerns about AI is still pending and would fit well here.}

## Data centers

**Prineville** has hosted large data centers since Facebook (now Meta) broke ground in 2010. Apple followed soon after. Meta’s Prineville campus has grown to 11 buildings covering 3.2 million square feet, and together the two companies have invested about $8 billion ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). {check: The outline said “since ~2012”; Marketplace dates the start to 2010.}

The data centers have supported massive improvements to the local economy. In 2010, Crook County’s unemployment rate was close to 20%. Today it is about 6%, and the county had the highest median private-sector wage in Oregon in 2024 ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). The city’s revenue is also projected to rise further as the companies’ property tax breaks begin to expire in 2027 (see the [Prineville case study](#case-data-centers)).

On the other hand, **La Pine** recently faced a heated debate over a proposal for a 20-megawatt data center to be used for AI and cryptocurrency (bitcoin) mining. After hours of public comment, the La Pine City Council rejected the project unanimously in May 2026 ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

**At the state level,** a first-of-its-kind report from ECOnorthwest and the University of Virginia found ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/17/oregon-data-centers-consume-quarter-state-power-report/)):

| Measure | Oregon today |
| --- | --- |
| Operating data centers | 111 |
| Planned or under construction | 32 more |
| Share of state’s retail electricity sales (2025) | About 23% |
| Projected share by 2030 | 31 to 32% |
| Direct data center workers | 2,630 (about 0.2% of the state workforce; 2,205 in Eastern Oregon) |

The state has recently put new rules in place to manage this growth. These include a pause on new enterprise-zone tax breaks for data centers (HB 4084, 2026), a separate electricity rate class for very large power users like data centers (the POWER Act, HB 3546, 2025), and a statewide Data Center Advisory Committee ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). ## Education

AI is being introduced into classrooms to support teachers, students, and administrators.

- **In Bend-La Pine Schools,** the district has procured MagicSchool AI. This includes tools to help teachers develop lesson plans and presentations. Students also have access to a chatbot that can help them work through problems or explore their curiosities. The district adopted an AI policy and an “AI Acceptable Use Scale” in 2025 ([Bend-La Pine Schools](https://www.blschools.org/teaching-and-learning/educational-technology/generative-ai)).
- **In Crook County,** the school board adopted an AI policy in March 2026 and blocked student access to generative AI until the policy was in place ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). {check: This comes from an AI-generated meeting summary; verify against official minutes.}
- **At Central Oregon Community College (COCC),** the college created an AI Committee in 2025 to recommend AI policies, review tools, and advise on AI-related cheating ([COCC](https://cocc.edu/policies/general-policy-manual/general/committee-structure/artificial-intelligence-committee)).

Oregon was also one of the first states to give school districts guidance on AI, through the Oregon Department of Education (ODE). Most of this guidance is about how to keep AI use ethical and responsible. That includes protecting student privacy, giving teachers discretion over AI use in their classrooms, and avoiding plagiarism detectors, which have been shown to be biased against multilingual students ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). According to content committee discussions, ODE’s guidance shifted over three versions, moving from encouraging AI use toward leaving decisions to local districts {src: CC Mtg 3, Oct 9}. {check: The outline says Oregon was “the first state.” Several sources describe Oregon as one of the first; confirm before using “first.”}

## Policing and surveillance

AI-powered cameras can help police departments collect data on people, cars, and activity in public spaces. **Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs)** have been the most recent subject of debate at the city and regional level. These cameras scan and catalog license plate numbers, along with a car’s make, model, and color.

- **Bend:** The Bend Police Department used grant funds to buy four stationary ALPR cameras from Flock Safety, a fast-growing company that uses AI to log data on all passing vehicles. The cameras sat at two Highway 97 intersections for about seven months as part of a pilot program. After packed city council meetings in early January 2026, the city decided to turn the cameras off and not renew the contract ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/01/08/bend-flock-cameras-ai-license-plate-camera-law-enforcement/)). Community advocacy from residents (a Change.org petition and testimony at council meetings) and local organizations (notably the Latino Community Association) drove the change. A key concern was whether federal immigration officials could access the data.
- **What happened next:** Bend began looking at a new vendor, Axon, for stationary ALPR cameras. After public pressure, the mayor committed in May 2026 to bring that decision to a council vote with public input ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)). {new: }
- **Deschutes County:** The county approved a $2.4 million contract with Axon for body cameras, dash cameras, and Tasers, but chose not to turn on the ALPR features ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/deschutes-county-sheriffs-office-to-contract-with-axon-for-body-dash-cams-and-tasers/)). {check: Year of this contract is inferred; confirm against the county commission agenda.}

## Government

Governments are using AI mostly behind the scenes, to help staff work faster. The table below shows how Oregon state agencies are using AI ([State uses, from Amy Paul](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1va1NYHXVXybVj7PxIA7K6IRd_El7Lhiz/view?usp=drive_link)).

::: wide
| Type of AI use | Example tool | Who can use it | Example uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Help with day-to-day tasks | Microsoft Copilot | Approved for all state employees | Summarizing emails, tracking follow-ups, research, brainstorming |
| AI features inside business software | Adobe, Gallup, Articulate | Approved case by case | Making training videos and captions; summarizing survey data |
| Custom-built AI tools | Built on generative AI models | Approved case by case, after a risk review | A chatbot that answers simple questions about new Medicaid work rules, so caseworkers can focus on harder questions |
:::

Public-facing AI tools used by state agencies must post a public “disclosure card” explaining what the tool does ([ODHS](https://www.oregon.gov/odhs/agency/Pages/ai-disclosure.aspx)).

**Locally,** governments are using AI chatbots to help staff draft and summarize emails, comb through documents, create content for press releases, and rewrite website language ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)):

- **City of Bend:** Staff use is limited to Microsoft 365 Copilot. About 400 of the city’s 800 employees have asked for it. Bend adopted an AI policy in 2025, along with a steering committee and staff training.
- **Deschutes County:** Staff use two chatbots, mostly for research and data analysis, under an AI policy adopted in 2024.

Local governments are not yet using AI tools that the public interacts with directly. Deschutes County is working toward a website chatbot, and Bend says it will look for “low risk, high value” uses first ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/ai-chatbots-assist-with-central-oregon-government-work/)). A content committee member also noted that the city is still looking for opportunities to use AI for public-facing purposes.

The content committee agreed on a scope line for the assembly: community input on AI tools that government uses *to serve the public* is in scope. Internal staff productivity tools (like drafting email with Copilot) are out of scope {src: CC Mtg 1, Sep 25}.
