Through our research, the Bend-La Pine School District stood out as one of the best local examples of thorough public engagement on how AI is used. {team: Unfinished in ORBB: “Karen Rush, …”}

The decision
: How AI may be used in classrooms by teachers and students, and which AI tools (like MagicSchool) the district uses

Who had authority
: The school board sets policy. The superintendent decides how the policy is carried out, through “administrative regulations” that don’t need board approval

The public’s role
: Stakeholder groups, public comment periods, school board meetings, and petitions

Competing views
: Many see AI as a useful tool for teachers and students. Some parents worry about screen time, privacy, and AI’s effect on learning

What happened afterward
: The district changed its policy to ban AI plagiarism detectors. After a parent petition, the board ordered a district-wide review of all classroom technology

**The district has many structures for public engagement.** It uses a very iterative process to get its policies right, plus forums for feedback with many different stakeholder groups {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}:

- **Student Voice Council**
- **Parent groups (PTAs)**
- **Specific committees** with parents and subject-matter experts (on technology, equity, and other topics) that help shape policy
- **Advocacy and equity coaches** who listen to community concerns and report back to the district’s policy director
- **School board meetings** and public comment periods

As Karen Rush of Bend-La Pine Schools put it: **“Our policies aren’t chiseled in stone. But the process is pretty clear.”**

**Public engagement on AI:**

- **Before:** The State of Oregon produced guidelines for AI use in school districts, which shaped the district’s starting point ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). Opportunities for public feedback at this stage were fairly limited, but it motivated the school district to form its own use policies.
- **During:** Bend-La Pine Schools engaged its stakeholder groups to shape its own policy. This included basic protections like student privacy and teacher discretion over AI use. Stakeholders raised concerns early on, for example that AI plagiarism detectors are unreliable and biased against students learning English as a second language. **The district made a real change here:** its policy now bans teachers from using AI plagiarism detectors {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}.
- **After:** Multiple points of public feedback pushed the district to make more changes:
  - A petition by Bend parents gathered over 1,100 signatures over concerns about AI and technology in classrooms, including MagicSchool. This led to community conversations and public comment, and in April 2026 the school board adopted a resolution to review all classroom technology and set grade-level technology standards ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-la-pine-schools-creates-policy-to-review-tech-in-classrooms/); [The Bulletin](https://bendbulletin.com/2026/02/11/bend-la-pine-parents-speak-up-about-ai-concerns/)). {check: Confirm the petition was specifically about MagicSchool, or about classroom technology more broadly.}
  - The Student Voice Council raised an issue they had with what they saw as a double standard: students were required to disclose their AI use, but teachers were not. This was amended in a later version of the district-wide use policy.

**Lessons and reflections {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}:**

- **Stakeholder groups are more siloed than the district would like.** The district also relies on the same engaged people again and again, which leads to burnout.
- **Representation is hard.** The district stretches from La Pine to Bend, and in-person meetings at the downtown Bend office are hard for rural families to attend. Student council members tend to be college-bound students, not a cross-section of all students.
- **It really matters to meet people where they are.** Ideas include going into community spaces, using text messages, and collecting student feedback at lunch instead of through online forms.
- **Processes for public engagement are a little too complex** and formal.

**Limits on what the public can change:** A completely “zero-tech” or “zero-AI” classroom isn’t realistic. Textbook publishers no longer print physical books, and state tests are now digital. Parents have also raised worries about voice-recognition software in state testing {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}.
