- Bend-La Pine Schools stood out as one of the best local examples of thorough public engagement on AI.
- Stakeholder feedback led the district to ban teachers from using AI plagiarism detectors.
- A parent petition with more than 1,100 signatures led the board to review all classroom technology.
- Lessons include being clear upfront about who decides, meeting people where they are, and avoiding burnout among the same few volunteers.
:::

Through our research, the Bend-La Pine School District stood out as one of the best local examples of thorough public engagement on how AI is used.

The decision
: How AI may be used in classrooms by teachers and students, and which AI tools (like MagicSchool) the district uses

Who had authority
: The school board sets policy. The superintendent decides how the policy is carried out, through “administrative regulations” that don’t need board approval

The public’s role
: Stakeholder groups, public comment periods, school board meetings, petitions, and appeals

Competing views
: Many see AI as a useful tool for teachers and students. Some parents worry about screen time, privacy, and AI’s effect on learning

What happened afterward
: The district changed its policy to ban AI plagiarism detectors. After a parent petition, the board ordered a district-wide review of all classroom technology

**The district has many structures for public engagement.** It uses a very iterative process to get its policies right, plus forums for feedback {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}:

- **Student Voice Council**
- **Parent groups (PTAs)**
- **Specific committees** with parents and subject-matter experts (on technology, equity, and other topics) that help shape policy
- **Advocacy and equity coaches** who listen to community concerns and report back to the district’s policy director
- **School board meetings** and public comment periods
- **An appeals process:** If a parent thinks a school isn’t following policy, they can appeal to the superintendent’s office and then to the school board

As Karen Rush of Bend-La Pine Schools put it: **“Our policies aren’t chiseled in stone. But the process is pretty clear.”**

**How a policy gets made {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}:**

1. State guidance (from ODE and the Oregon School Boards Association) informs the starting point.
2. The district’s policy director writes a first draft.
3. Expert groups (like the equity coalition) review the draft.
4. The draft goes to the school board and a public comment period.
5. Controversial policies can go through many rounds. One recent policy took about nine months.

**Public engagement on AI:**

- **Before:** The State of Oregon produced guidelines for AI use in school districts, which shaped the district’s starting point ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). {team: It is unclear whether the public had opportunities to weigh in on the state guidance.}
- **During:** Bend-La Pine Schools engaged its stakeholder groups to shape its own policy. This included basic protections like student privacy and teacher discretion over AI use. Stakeholders raised concerns early on, for example that AI plagiarism detectors are unreliable and biased against students learning English as a second language. **The district made a real change here:** its policy now bans teachers from using AI plagiarism detectors {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}.
- **After:** Multiple points of public feedback pushed the district to make more changes:
  - Proactive engagement with student advisory boards.
  - A petition by Bend parents gathered over 1,100 signatures over concerns about AI and technology in classrooms, including MagicSchool. This led to community conversations and public comment, and in April 2026 the school board adopted a resolution to review all classroom technology and set grade-level technology standards ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-la-pine-schools-creates-policy-to-review-tech-in-classrooms/); [The Bulletin](https://bendbulletin.com/2026/02/11/bend-la-pine-parents-speak-up-about-ai-concerns/)). {check: Confirm the petition was specifically about MagicSchool, as the outline says, or about classroom technology more broadly.}

**Limits on what the public can change:** A completely “zero-tech” or “zero-AI” classroom isn’t realistic. Textbook publishers no longer print physical books, and state tests are now digital. Parents have also raised worries about voice-recognition software in state testing {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}.

**Lessons and reflections {src: Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15}:**

- **Be clear upfront about who decides.** People in advisory groups were frustrated when they didn’t realize their role was only advisory.
- **Stakeholder groups are more siloed than the district would like.** The district also relies on the same engaged people again and again, which leads to burnout.
- **Representation is hard.** The district stretches from La Pine to Bend, and in-person meetings at the downtown Bend office are hard for rural families to attend. Student council members tend to be college-bound students, not a cross-section of all students.
- **It really matters to meet people where they are.** Ideas include going into community spaces, using text messages, and collecting student feedback at lunch instead of through online forms.
- **Processes for public engagement are a little too complex** and formal.

**What the district wants to learn from the community:** how to define transparency, how to build better feedback loops, and how children are using AI at home, so the district can partner with families on student safety.
