# **AI-Related Decisions in Central Oregon**

## What are we talking about when we say "AI"?

In short, artificial intelligence (AI) is "a broad term for computer systems that can perform tasks with human-like intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing images, learning from data, reasoning, and making decisions" ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-definitions/what-is-artificial-intelligence-ai)).

Unlike traditional software, which follows rules written step by step by programmers, modern AI systems "learn" from data. They find patterns across millions or billions of examples and use those patterns to make predictions or responses in new situations. In some cases, they can be enabled to complete tasks with minimal human supervision.

| Before  | Now |
| :---- | :---- |
| Specific AI tools were already being used daily in a variety of ways(e.g., Google search, spam filters, GPS, voice assistants, etc).  | Flexible generative models (e.g., LLMs/ChatGPT) that create text, code, and media directly from user prompts, and are able to act autonomously. |

### **The core technology**

Most of today's AI tools run on **"models”.** A model is essentially just very complex statistics; computers (whether on your phone, an on-site server, or a data center) are able to use these models to respond to users’ requests. To do this, they use immense amounts of data to interpret queries and determine what the most logical steps are to complete a task.

* **Who builds them:** Models are developed by AI research labs and large corporations, such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and Microsoft.  
* **What they need:** Building (or "training") a model requires a massive amount of data, along with huge amounts of computing power and electricity.  
* **How we use them:** Most people never touch a model directly. Instead, they use an app (like ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or a school tool like MagicSchool) that sends their request to a model running in a data center somewhere else.

### **What can it do?**

| Kind of work | Examples |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Standard tasks** | Drafting emails, summarizing long documents, creating spreadsheets, taking meeting notes |
| **Research** | Producing research reports, analyzing data, answering questions about a large set of documents. Scientists and mathematicians are also using Large Language Models to accelerate scientific research, such as inventing new drugs, solving fluid mechanics problems, or reading mammograms to detect cancer. |
| **Creative work** | Producing images and videos, or helping with key steps in an artist's workflow that aren't just producing the art itself (like brainstorming, editing, or captioning) |

**What infrastructure does it take?** 

Data centers are not new; they have been the backbone of the internet for decades. They do three main jobs:

1. **Store and retrieve data ("cloud computing").** This is the most familiar use of data centers, and what the current AI industry is built on. Even before the AI industry took off, data centers were needed to store the data for websites, stream videos on sites like YouTube, and back up photos and email. Over the past few decades, more and more of our information has been stored in data centers.  
2. **Run calculations for everyday apps ("inference").** Data centers have always done the computing behind everyday apps. When you search for something on Google, the algorithms that power the search run on a computer in a data center. Each time someone asks an AI chatbot a question, a data center does that work too.  
3. **Train AI models.** This is the newest use for data centers. Training a large model can take thousands of specialized chips running nonstop for weeks or months.

## What AI-related decisions are happening in Central Oregon? 

AI is already part of daily life in Central Oregon, sometimes in ways that we know about, and sometimes in ways that are harder to see. It shows up in local businesses, in the region's growing number of data centers, in classrooms, in policing, and inside government offices.

What we are most focused on during this Assembly are the **decisions that the government is making related to AI, and how the public can influence those decisions.** For example, an issue like “how to manage AI in the classroom” involves the school boards across the region creating new technology use policies on the topic, with some guidance from the state Department of Education. We found many examples of these decisions at various levels of government, including: 

* The City of Bend developing guidance for how city workers use AI, and figuring out which uses of AI are desired and beneficial for the public  
* Multiple school boards procuring new AI to support educational outcomes in the classroom  
* The City of Prineville discussing new data centers in its economic planning strategies, and how additional revenue from these projects should be used  
* Organizers in Bend turning out to shut down the use of AI-powered surveillance systems in their city  
* And many more. 

**We have broken down these decisions into three types of decisions: what AI is used for, planning decisions, and restrictive regulations.** As you think about the AI-related issues that matter most to you, try to identify where they fit in this map. 

| Type | What government is deciding | Key Opportunities | Where the public usually fits in |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Uses of AI Technology** | Whether and how government itself uses AI tools (for classroom support, public benefits, application review, etc).  | Requests for qualifications, contracts, AI use policies | Discussions around use policies may happen, but engagement on procurement decisions is rare |
| **Permissive Planning** | Whether and where private AI investments (like data centers) can locate, and on what terms | Economic plans, comprehensive plans, growth boundaries, permits, tax agreements | Hearings on long-range plans and specific projects |
| **Restrictive Regulations** | How to rein in possible harms from private industry | Laws, ordinances, administrative rules, utility orders | Agenda setting, advisory bodies, public hearings |

## Government Use (Procurement, AI use policies)

| Who’s involved? The City Manager, School Boards, and other city staff are involved. |
| :---- |
| **What rules are already in place?** Public Invitations to Bid and Requests for Solicitation are currently used. |
| **What are the potential impacts, tradeoffs, etc?** Increased speed and public service capacity must be balanced against privacy and accuracy concerns. |
| **When/Where could the public have a say?** The public can shape procurement goals, participate in evaluations, and provide feedback on technology impacts. |
| **Case Study:** Link to schools case study  |

As described above, government agencies are already using AI tools, mostly to help staff work faster. The rationale for the public sector to use these systems is similar to the private sector: increase efficiency and speed of transactions and lower the costs (Hodjick 2022). 

**Procurement** is the process the government uses to buy goods and services. Larger purchases are usually defined through a **Request for Qualifications (RFQ)** or similar request, which describes what the government needs; these documents are usually available online, before vendors apply and are selected. Final purchasing decisions are settled through a **contract** with the company providing the services. 

Here are some examples: 

| Tool | Who bought it | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Microsoft Copilot | City of Bend | Only AI chatbot approved for city staff |
| Flock Safety license plate cameras | Bend Police Department | Bought with grant funds; contract not renewed after public pushback |
| MagicSchool AI | Bend-La Pine Schools | Procurement guided by state (ODE) guidelines |

**Throughout these processes, transparency is generally required to preserve trust and accountability with the public.** Secrecy in public contracting can lead to waste, corruption, and loss of public trust ([OGP, The Costs of Secrecy](https://www.opengovpartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/OGP_Costs-of-Secrecy_20180515.pdf)). Bid documents are typically posted for the public on portals like [oregonbids.com](http://oregonbids.com) (see the [City of Bend example](https://www.oregonbids.com/government-agencies/deschutes/bend-city-of-41286/)). **Small purchases may skip public review entirely,** as staff can buy things under their own purchasing authority (this was the case with the Flock cameras). Some experts argue procurement rules should require more from AI companies, such as details of data qualities, model decision decisions, optimization techniques and processes when required by the public entity. 

**Opportunities for public feedback during these processes** (e.g., public comment, voting, and other public input) is generally uncommon, for some good reasons:

* **Keeping the market fair.** Governments don't want to give one supplier an unfair advantage, or force a private company to disclose information that would put it at a disadvantage compared to its competitors.  
* **Workload.** Opening every purchase to public input would overwhelm staff, taking time away from other important work.  
* **Technical expertise.** Many details of the services being bought require technical expertise that most residents (and even many officials) don't have. 

* On the other hand, governments can create **oversight bodies** to watch over certain kinds of purchases. Examples include public policing commissions, or Bend's Human Rights and Equity Commission, which received updates on the Flock decision ([City of Bend HREC minutes, Jan 13, 2026](https://bendoregon.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/HREC-1.13.2026-Meeting-Minutes-Draft_Lisa-Larsen.pdf)). 

**After purchasing decisions are made,** the public can still have influence. Contracts come up for renewal, and councils can review them. In Bend, public pressure led the city not to renew the Flock contract, and later to promise a council vote and public input before adding new license plate cameras from Axon ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/)).

**AI use policies** set the rules for how government workers, school community members, or otherwise may use AI to improve their work. This is another opportunity to shape how these technologies impact the lives of residents. 

* **In education,** many guidelines come down from higher levels of government. For example, the Oregon Department of Education created thorough guidelines for districts ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). Districts then write their own policies (see the Bend-La Pine case study).  
* **In state government,** agencies follow a statewide Responsible AI Usage Policy and an AI risk management framework. Public-facing AI tools must post a disclosure card ([State uses, from Amy Paul](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1va1NYHXVXybVj7PxIA7K6IRd_El7Lhiz/view?usp=drive_link)).  
* **In local government,** policies like Bend's are usually set by the city manager as administrative rules. The Bulletin's editorial board said the public had no chance to weigh in on Bend's policy ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). 

## Planning & Permissions

| What does it govern? Data centers, permits for power lines, land easements, etc.  |
| :---- |
| **Who’s involved?** Planning Commissions, the Economic Development Corporation of Central Oregon (EDCO), and COIC are involved in data center siting decisions. |
| **What rules are already in place?** Oregon state laws govern community benefits in exchange for tax abatements, general plans guide land use, and most industrial siting decisions are handled through corporate-city agreements. |
| **What are the potential impacts and tradeoffs?** Attracting large industrial players (e.g., AI companies, data center operators) brings economic and community benefits, but also raises concerns regarding energy usage, noise, and broader environmental impacts. |
| **When/Where could the public have a say?** The public can participate through comments on general plans, codesign opportunities, and input on community benefit agreements or revenue allocation. |
| **Case Study:** (Link to Data Centers Case Study) |

In the local context, this is most relevant to data centers and other physical AI investments. The goal of these decisions is to figure out *whether, where, and on what terms* a community wants from new development.

**At the state level,** a first-of-its-kind [report from ECOnorthwest and the University of Virginia](https://econw.com/wp-content/uploads/ECOnorthwest_Understanding-Oregons-Data-Center-Industry_Full-Report.pdf) found the following: 

| Measure | Oregon today |
| :---- | :---- |
| Operating data centers | 111 |
| Planned or under construction | 32 more |
| Share of state's retail electricity sales (2025) | About 23% |
| Projected share by 2030 | 31 to 32% |
| Direct data center workers | 2,630 (about 0.2% of the state workforce; 2,205 in Eastern Oregon) |

The state has recently put new rules in place to manage this growth. These include a pause on new enterprise-zone tax breaks for data centers (HB 4084, 2026), a separate electricity rate class for very large power users like data centers (the POWER Act, HB 3546, 2025), and a statewide Data Center Advisory Committee ([Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)). 

**Planning** sets the long-term vision for a community's economy and land, as well as policies that might encourage (or discourage) certain kinds of economic growth. This may include things like:

* **Comprehensive (general) plans** set the overall vision and rules for land use in a city or county. These are often developed by the Planning Department of the city or county.   
* **Economic Development Strategy (EDS):** A regional economic plan developed by extragovernmental entities, like the Economic Development Corporation of Central Oregon or the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council ([COIC](https://www.coic.org/ceds/)). These plans typically include key areas of investment,   
* **Economic Opportunities Analysis (EOA):** Oregon's land use rules require cities to plan for enough land and economic opportunities to support their expected growth over the next 20 years. Economic Development for Central Oregon (EDCO) helps local governments develop these analyses.   
* **Enterprise development zones,** where special tax breaks, regulatory relief, or otherwise might make it easy for larger corporations to bring labor, tax revenue, etc to the region.   
* **Urban Growth Boundary (UGB) adjustments** decide where a city can grow. In Oregon, a UGB expansion needs approval from both the city and the county.

**Before these plans are established:** It is common practice for planning departments to do thorough community engagement when shaping up larger planning documents and strategies. This helps to align the plans for the city or county with the community’s vision for where they live, their values, the risks that they see, and more. The extent of engagement varies based on the entity involved and the type of planning document that they are producing: 

- EDCO engages residents in the region when it refreshes its Economic Development Strategy every three years.  
- The city of Arcata, CA engaged its residents in a lengthy community engagement process when creating its economic development plan.   
- It is common for cities and counties to engage their residents in years-long engagement processes to develop their general plans, which include things like zoning decisions (which kinds of businesses are allowed to be where), investment priorities, transportation planning, and more. 

**As the plans are actually developed, there are usually smaller opportunities for the public to weigh in.** In some cases, committees or advisory boards might be established to help provide real-time feedback as the details of the planning documents are established. At the very least, most of these documents are officially adopted by the local governments, who may hold public meetings to discuss the details of the plans before they are officially voted on. In some cases, residents have organized petitions against certain aspects of a plan (e.g., urban growth boundaries in Prineville), which have motivated changes in the scope or details of planning decisions. 

**Afterwards, both public and private entities use the plans to guide their decision-making.** The city will often use these plans to guide its investments and industrial policies. Businesses (both local businesses and larger) will often base their decisions. These decisions can have a significant

While the public may have some opportunities for feedback in the earlier stages, decisions with larger corporations and developers are more likely to depend on private negotiations between the city/county and that private entity. These decisions might include things like: 

* **Community Benefit Agreements (CBAs)**: are basically a way of saying, "You can come here on these conditions, to make sure you help our community thrive." This might include everything from the corporation paying for essential infrastructure upgrades to providing grants for public schools, hiring locals for projects, or otherwise. They are negotiated between the city/county and the corporation, and are made official through a contract. Some processes for determining community benefit agreements are more inclusive of the community than others.   
* **Incentives:** In order to bring Meta and Apple to Prineville, the city’s leaders waived property taxes for these corporations for 15 years. 

**These discussions are usually much more private.** Companies will often establish nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) with the city to protect their strategic plans, financials, and otherwise from giving their competitors a leg up; this also prevents the public from knowing many details about these plans before they are established. Recently, companies like Amazon have ended their practice of establishing NDAs after widespread public outcry. A key challenge is to figure out what information the public needs to know (and when) in order to guide decisions in their best interest. 

## Regulations

| Who’s involved? City Councils, County Commissions, and the State Legislature are involved in setting regulations. |
| :---- |
| **What rules are already in place?** Public comment processes are currently used during regulatory decisions. |
| **What are the potential impacts, tradeoffs, etc?** Regulations protect the public interest but require balancing effective oversight against overly restrictive rules given emerging technology uncertainties. |
| **How can the public have a say?** The public can participate through council meeting comments, advocacy, and proposing community-informed policies. |
| **Case Study:** (Link to Bend Case Study) |

Regulations help to rein in the potential negative effects of private industry, and place guardrails, guidance, etc around the actions of government, corporate, and individual actors to serve the public interest. 

**Examples:**

* **Data center moratoriums** (temporary bans) at the local level, passed by city councils. Hillsboro, Salem, and Woodburn have passed them ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/09/15/woodburn-moratorium-new-data-centers/)). At the state level, the Governor has paused data center permits until July 2027\.   
* **Limits on data center tax breaks** passed by the Oregon Legislature (HB 4084, 2026).  
* **Rules on electricity costs** set by the Oregon PUC under the POWER Act (2025), so that very large users pay for the new power infrastructure they need.  
* **License plate reader rules** at the state level, which then shape local police purchasing decisions. Senate Bill 1516, signed in March 2026, added requirements like end-to-end encryption, and a statewide ban on Flock-type cameras has been proposed for 2027 ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-officials-commit-to-council-vote-public-discussion-regarding-stationary-surveillance-camera-contract-add-on/); [Central Oregon Decisions tracker](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19zmUMXSqIPJhdJZTfiAFqM_DHhTYhB1pbOovrmJVmks/edit?gid=1770817539#gid=1770817539)).

Governor Kotek recently announced a 60 day deadline for the state legislature to pass more informed regulations ([Oregon Capital Chronicle](https://oregoncapitalchronicle.com/2026/11/06/oregon-gov-kotek-says-data-center-rules-universal-preschool-among-priorities-for-next-4-years/)). This situation is actively evolving and more news is likely to emerge in the coming weeks and months. 

**Public engagement in regulation:**

* **Advocacy and Agenda Setting.** Advocacy from residents is often what motivates new rules. This can include petitions, advocacy groups, and research that gathers evidence of potential harms. This is usually mediated through more organized and well-resourced organizations that have the time, capacity, connections, and otherwise to reach public officials.   
* **Public hearings** at city council, the legislature, or through online processes are also good opportunities to weigh in.  
* **Public advisory bodies** can be formed to help guide what and how to regulate. Oregon's Data Center Advisory Committee is a good example. It heard testimony from local leaders (including Prineville's) and took public comment on its preliminary findings through October 24, 2026 (EDCO written submission, Oct 13). Newer innovations like civic assemblies can also be considered advisory or consultative bodies.

## What makes AI-related decisions different from other government decisions?

In some ways, AI-related decisions are not very different from other decisions government agencies make. It is generally helpful to compare AI to other major industries, like energy (oil, gas, hydropower, renewables). Like a new power plant or factory, a data center brings jobs and tax revenue but also uses land, water, and electricity. Local officials have pointed out that Prineville's existing data centers are not AI data centers, and that many of the same land use and tax tools apply (CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14).

However, it's worth calling out a few things that make this industry different.

### **Speed: technology moves faster than the rules**

The speed of technological progress outpaces our ability to regulate it. Researchers call this the **Collingridge dilemma**, named after a 1980 book on controlling technology. It describes a double bind: "Early on, intervention is possible but impacts are uncertain. Once impacts become clear, the technology is entrenched" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingridge_dilemma), summarizing *The Social Control of Technology*). This is closely related to the "pacing problem," the idea that new technology increasingly outpaces laws and regulations.

Content committee member Rachel Haakenson put it this way: regulation tends to follow adoption, which creates the feeling of a race (CC Mtg 1, Sep 25).

**The speed and size of investment is larger than anything we've seen before.** One Brookings analysis projects that investment in AI data center buildings, power systems, networks, and specialized chips will total \$10.3 trillion from 2025 to 2032\. That's an average of 3.63% of U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) every year. "The projected buildout would be larger relative to the economy than the major U.S. canal, railroad, electrification, highway, and telecommunications investment booms," writes the author, Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh of Columbia University ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/financing-the-ai-buildout/)).

Massive, speedy investment also means that local governments may feel they can't afford to be "left behind" in the wave of technological progress and economic growth. For many, this feels like a pivotal moment. At the same time, content committee member Robin Hayakawa noted that community-facing AI in local government does not need to be rushed, and that residents should always be able to reach a real person (CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14).

### **Private development: public input can be limited or outcompeted**

**The "black box" challenge.** It is hard to know exactly how AI systems work, what data they were (or were not) trained on, and why they give the answers they do.

* **Many layers between the community and the model.** Most AI tools are provided through private service providers, who themselves depend on AI models built by a few very large companies (like Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft). This makes it difficult to change how the models behave to meet the specific needs or concerns of local residents.  
* **It doesn't have to be this way.** Communities and governments can also use locally governed models or "open-weight" models, whose inner workings can be inspected and adjusted.  
* **Some things can't be taken back.** Content committee member Marcio Nunes noted that institutional knowledge fed into shared AI systems can't be "un-taught," which raises questions about ownership and trust (CC Mtg 1, Sep 25).

**Well-funded companies can engage governments in ways regular residents can't.** High investment in AI companies often means they have the time, budget, and skill to work with city and regional governments far more than everyday residents can. Companies like Waymo and Flock have invested heavily in public relations and government outreach. Playbooks for winning local approval are being developed in a way that feels notably different from earlier industrial revolutions. **\[CHECK\]** This claim needs a source (for example, reporting on Flock's or Waymo's lobbying and public relations spending).

Communities are developing playbooks too. Data Center Watch describes local opposition groups as having "internalized an opposition playbook," sharing tactics across 49 states ([Data Center Watch](https://datacenterwatch.org/q1-2026)). 

### **Loss of agency**

* The way AI systems are deployed often means **automating human judgment**: decisions once made by a person (a teacher, a clerk, a police officer) are now shaped or made by software.  
* Combined with the speed of development and the presence of well-financed private companies, many people feel a profound **loss of agency** with the arrival of the AI industry. In Oregon, about 60% of voters report negative feelings about AI ([KLCC/OPB](https://www.klcc.org/politics-government/2026-08-06/oregonians-are-extremely-skeptical-of-data-centers-new-polling-suggests)).

## Case studies

The case studies below show how real AI-related decisions have played out in Central Oregon. For each one, we look at:

* **The decision:** What was being decided?  
* **Who had authority:** Who made the final call?  
* **The public's role:** When and where could the public influence it?  
* **Competing views:** What did different people think?  
* **What happened afterward?**

### Case 1: Bend-La Pine School District's AI use policy

\<\< picture of classroom, maybe Karen Rush? \>\> 

Through our research, the Bend-La Pine School District stood out as a great local example of thorough public engagement on how AI is used. 

**The district has many structures for public engagement.** It uses a very iterative process to get its policies right, plus forums for feedback with many different stakeholder groups: 

* **Student Voice Council,** an advisory body of representatives of the student body  
* **Parent groups (PTAs)**  
* **Specific committees** with parents and subject-matter experts (on technology, equity, and other topics) that help shape policy  
* **Advocacy and equity coaches** who listen to community concerns and report back to the district's policy director  
* **School board meetings** and public comment periods

As Karen Rush of Bend-La Pine Schools put it: **"Our policies aren't chiseled in stone. But the process is pretty clear."**

**Public engagement on AI:**

* **Before:** The State of Oregon produced guidelines for AI use in school districts, which shaped the district's starting point ([ODE](https://www.oregon.gov/ode/educator-resources/teachingcontent/pages/generative-artificial-intelligence-%28ai%29-for-k-12-schools.aspx)). Opportunities for public feedback at this stage were fairly limited, but it motivated the school district to form its own use policies.   
* **During:** Bend-La Pine Schools engaged its stakeholder groups to shape its own policy. This included basic protections like student privacy and teacher discretion over AI use. Stakeholders raised concerns early on, for example that AI plagiarism detectors are unreliable and biased against students learning English as a second language. **The district made a real change here:** its policy now bans teachers from using AI plagiarism detectors (Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15).  
* **After:** Multiple points of public feedback pushed the district to make more changes:  
  * A petition by Bend parents gathered over 1,100 signatures over concerns about AI and technology in classrooms, including MagicSchool. This led to community conversations and public comment, and in April 2026 the school board adopted a resolution to review all classroom technology and set grade-level technology standards ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/bend-la-pine-schools-creates-policy-to-review-tech-in-classrooms/); [The Bulletin](https://bendbulletin.com/2026/02/11/bend-la-pine-parents-speak-up-about-ai-concerns/)).   
  * The Student Voice Council raised an issue they had with what they saw as a double-standard: the fact that students were required to disclose their AI use, but teachers were not. This was amended in a future version of their district-wide use policy. 

**Lessons and reflections (Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15):**

* **Stakeholder groups are more siloed than the district would like.** The district also relies on the same engaged people again and again, which leads to burnout.  
* **Representation is hard.** The district stretches from La Pine to Bend, and in-person meetings at the downtown Bend office are hard for rural families to attend. Student council members tend to be college-bound students, not a cross-section of all students.  
* **It really matters to meet people where they are.** Ideas include going into community spaces, using text messages, and collecting student feedback at lunch instead of through online forms.  
* **Processes for public engagement are a little too complex** and formal.

* **Limits on what the public can change:** A completely "zero-tech" or "zero-AI" classroom isn't realistic. Textbook publishers no longer print physical books, and state tests are now digital. Parents have also raised worries about voice-recognition software in state testing (Karen Rush meeting, Oct 15).

### **Case 2: Data centers in Prineville vs. La Pine**

Neither of these cases involves a large "AI data center" of the newest kind; Prineville's existing data centers were built mostly for cloud computing, and La Pine's proposal was mainly for cryptocurrency mining. However, they are good examples of how communities make decisions about data centers, and they show two very different outcomes. 

#### **Prineville**

In the city's own words, Prineville was "dying on the vine" following the housing crash and didn't have other options knocking on its door ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)). When Meta first approached the city, they pursued a number of economic arrangements to make sure the data centers would benefit the community, including franchise fees on electricity (about \$1 million per month today) and company-funded water storage and wastewater projects ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2026/10/21/apple-and-meta-data-centers-divide-oregon-towns-residents)).

**Despite the apparent success, there was not much public engagement:**

* The work was led mainly by the city's economic development department, EDCO, and consultants.  
* There was little public pushback at the time. This highlights how today feels different from earlier waves of investment, when data centers served uses people accepted more readily (cloud storage, video streaming, recommendation algorithms).  
* Today, residents are more divided. Some credit the data centers for good jobs. Others say the facilities raised the cost of living, light up the night sky, and should be shared with other communities rather than concentrated in one place. 

**Now, the city says it is in a position to demand more from AI companies,** including and beyond Meta ([The Source](https://www.bendsource.com/news/localnews/prineville-wants-more-out-of-its-next-data-center-deal/)). The 2026 economic plan includes data centers as a potential investment, citing the benefits they brought before. It found the city is out of large industrial sites and needs about 755 more acres (Central Oregon Decisions tracker).

**However, the biggest fight in this recent planning review wasn't about data centers –** it was about expanding the urban growth boundary for a biomass power plant on what had been farmland. Most city and county officials pointed out that the city's growth has depended on steady, strategic boundary adjustments, while residents argued that converting farmland goes against their community's values. The city later resumed its plan without fully addressing the biomass project or urban growth boundary issue.

#### **La Pine**

In May 2026, the La Pine City Council unanimously rejected a proposed land sale of approximately 20 acres in the La Pine Industrial Park to Boxminer.io for a 20-megawatt data center. Although the developer promised local jobs without inflating power bills or water usage, residents expressed strong opposition due to concerns over noise, community impacts, and potential strains on the rural power grid and water resources, leading to the decision following public outcry.
