    What is the goal of public engagement?

    Key points

    • About 266,000 people live in Crook, Deschutes, and Jefferson counties, and state and local government shapes nearly every part of daily life, from land and roads to schools and public health.
    • Decisions about AI land in many of the same places: school boards, city councils, county commissions, and state agencies.
    • Voting is only the beginning. Between elections, public bodies are expected to hold open forums and invite people to weigh in.
    • This section describes how engagement works today. What counts as “good” engagement is left to delegates.

    About 266,000 people live in Crook, Deschutes, and Jefferson counties, and nearly every part of their daily lives is shaped by state and local government. (Census, July 2025 estimates: Deschutes 213,072; Crook 27,564; Jefferson about 25,600.) Verify Jefferson County figure

    Part of daily lifeWho makes the decisions
    Land and natural resourcesMost land in the region is public, almost all of it federal: more than 77% of Deschutes County and more than half of Crook County (USDA NRCS, Deschutes; Crook). It is managed by the Deschutes National Forest, Ochoco National Forest, and BLM Prineville District. The Oregon Department of Forestry runs its Central Oregon District from Prineville.

    Public healthEach county runs a health department (Deschutes, Crook, Jefferson), overseen by the Oregon Health Authority.
    EconomyCities and counties handle planning, permits, and incentives like enterprise zones. The state works in the region through the Governor’s Central Oregon Regional Solutions team. The nonprofit Economic Development for Central Oregon (EDCO) recruits and supports businesses.

    Decisions about artificial intelligence are no exception. They land in many of these same places: school boards set AI use policies, cities and counties approve data center sites and tax incentives, and state agencies set rules for their own use of AI. (See What is the range of AI-related decisions?)

    Because we live in a democracy, these decisions should be made with input from the people they affect. When people think about democracy, they often think about elections: voting for the mayors, commissioners, school board members, and other officials who represent us. Voting is necessary, but it is only the beginning. Between elections, public bodies are expected to hold open forums and invite people to weigh in.

    Effective public engagement helps ensure that public sector decisions are informed not only by technical expertise, but also by the knowledge, values, priorities, and personal experiences of the people who will be affected by those decisions and whose tax dollars support them. Utah book

    Examples of public consultation include Utah book:

    • State legislative hearings, and city council and county commission meetings that are open to the public and invite public comment
    • Town halls on specific topics
    • Public comment periods when local or state rules are being drafted
    • Public meetings organized by local or state elected officials
    • And, of course, elections

    Note: This section describes how engagement works today. It does not define what “good” engagement means; that is left to delegates.

    Who is involved?

    Key points

    • Government makes the decision, but residents, organized groups, advocacy groups, and businesses all try to shape it.
    • Residents who organize tend to have more influence, because officials see them as speaking for a broader group.
    • Business deals are often harder for the public to see. Early recruitment is often confidential, and companies may hire lobbyists.
    • Lobbyists who work with the Legislature or state officials must register and file reports anyone can search.

    How the layers of government fit together

    Key points

    • Five layers of government overlap in Central Oregon: state, county, city, special districts, and the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs.
    • The state sets the rules counties and cities must follow. The federal government manages much of the region’s land.
    • Residents of unincorporated places like Sunriver or Terrebonne have no city council, so the county commission and district boards are their main local voices.
    • Federal agencies must consult with Tribes, government to government, before decisions that affect them.

    Several layers of government overlap in Central Oregon, and each has its own job. The state sets the rules that counties and cities must follow. The federal government sits above all of them and manages much of the region’s land, but this book focuses on state and local decisions. Layers of Government in Oregon; Who Governs Central Oregon

    Cities. Central Oregon has eight cities: Bend, Redmond, Sisters, and La Pine (Deschutes County); Prineville (Crook County); and Madras, Culver, and Metolius (Jefferson County). Every city has an elected council that sets policy and adopts the budget. Many use a professional manager or administrator to run day-to-day work. Verify for each city Fire, libraries, and much of parks and recreation come from special districts, and the county sheriff handles policing in some smaller cities.

    Rural, unincorporated areas. Many people live outside the eight cities, in places like Sunriver, Terrebonne, Tumalo, Crooked River Ranch, and Camp Sherman. These areas are served mainly by the county, plus any special districts that cover them. Residents there have no city council, so their main local voices are the county commission and district boards.

    Regional and education bodies. Some bodies cover parts of several counties, like the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council (COIC). Public schools, community colleges, and universities also have their own boards and decision-making power, within state and federal law.

    Warm Springs Reservation. When federal agencies make decisions that affect Tribes, they must consult with them. The Bureau of Indian Affairs describes Tribal consultation as a formal, two-way, government-to-government conversation held before a decision. It calls for at least 30 days’ notice, decision-makers taking part, a record of what was said, and a response explaining how Tribal input was used. For Oregon state agencies, see ORS 182.162 to 182.168.

    How and when can the public get involved?

    Key points

    • Engagement can happen before a decision, while it’s being made, and after it takes effect.
    • Groups outside government work at every stage, from early meetings with officials to appeals and ballot campaigns.
    • Having a chance to engage isn’t the same as being able to use it. Schedules, language, distance, internet access, and trust all get in the way.
    • For every stage, ask: who is missing, and who keeps getting asked?

    Before a decision: notice, education, due diligence

    Key points

    • Notice: people need to hear about decisions early enough to contribute. Oregon law requires meeting notices and mailed notices for many land use hearings, but renters and people farther away can be missed.
    • Education: accurate, balanced information helps people push for what helps and against real harms. Committee members disagreed about how much opposition comes from misunderstanding.
    • Due diligence: decision-makers need both the facts and a sense of what the public wants.
    • Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change.

    Notice

    People need to hear about decisions that matter to them early enough to contribute.

    Why it matters. Simply put, you can’t weigh in on something you don’t know about. When people are left out, government can make decisions that accidentally harm them, and trust drops, which makes compromise harder later. Local leaders said people are often brought in only after a decision is mostly made. Rachel Haakenson (NeighborImpact) noted that much is decided before feedback is invited, and that engagement after a plan exists becomes endorsement. Kathi Barguil (Mixte Communications) said engagement should start at the very beginning, with the community helping define the problem and who is affected. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14

    What it looks like.

    • Meeting notices. Oregon’s Public Meetings Law requires notice of every meeting of a governing body: at least 48 hours ahead for regular meetings and 24 hours for special ones. Notices must list the date, time, place, how to join online, and the main topics. Added City of Springfield summary of OGEC training, Dec 2025
    • Mailing lists. Many local governments let you sign up to get public notices and agendas by email, including Crook County and the City of Prineville.
    • Land use notices. Before many land use hearings, cities and counties must mail notice at least 20 days ahead to nearby property owners: within 100 feet inside an urban growth boundary, 250 feet outside one, and 750 feet in farm or forest zones. Renters and people farther away may not get a letter. Added ORS 197.763

    Education

    People need accurate, balanced information to take part in a meaningful way.

    Why it matters. Well-informed people can push for what will truly help them and against real harms. This includes learning about the topic itself (for example, data centers), what decision is being made, who makes it, and on what timeline. Several speakers raised misinformation. Teresa Coats said people need a basic understanding of AI before deciding, since views shaped by the news, friends, or a layoff can lead to snap decisions. Adam Young pointed to a local claim that Ochoco Reservoir is low because of data centers as a reason for myth-versus-fact resources. Others pushed back on this framing: Robin Hayakawa questioned the idea that opposition comes from not understanding AI, and Taha Elwefati said the knowledge gap runs both ways. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14

    What it looks like.

    • Policy explainers, local news and civic media, and sit-downs with experts
    • Media literacy
    • Learning from places that have faced similar problems
    • Asking specific questions. Instead of only asking “data center or no data center,” Adam Young suggested asking where it would go and what its effects would be on the environment, light, and noise. Member input v.3, Oct 7
    • Balanced education sessions before public comment, which Taha Elwefati suggested may be a missing step delegates could recommend. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9

    Challenges. Good information often has to be pulled from many sources. Robin Hayakawa noted it is hard to expect people to dig into heavy, technical issues “unless it was their job to understand them,” which leaves residents relying on elected and appointed officials. Member input v.3, Oct 7

    Due diligence

    Decision-makers need good information from all sides, and they need to weigh the possible effects on the community.

    Why it matters. Public officials are expected to explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, and report on results. Knowing they will be expected to do so reinforces the importance of careful decision-making and stewardship of public funds, as well as responsiveness to the people they serve. Utah book

    What it looks like. Officials often ask for reports, briefings, and expert advice, and staff help produce them and connect officials with experts. This work has two parts: the facts (what is actually happening?) and public preferences (what do people actually want?). Locally, the City of Prineville says it treats data centers like any large industrial project: study the demand, check whether there is enough capacity, then negotiate development agreements and incentives. CC Mtg 2, Oct 1

    Challenges. Some decisions are already fixed by law, so asking the public about them is just box-checking. Engagement works best when it focuses on what can actually change. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9 Verify Rachel Haakenson or Taha Elwefati

    During a decision: feedback and deliberation

    Key points

    • Feedback lets the public weigh in before a policy is final, through comment periods, hearings, surveys, and advisory councils.
    • Local examples show feedback changing outcomes, from Bend-La Pine’s classroom AI rules to new transit service in Madras and Bend.
    • Comment processes often favor the most motivated voices, and the same few people get asked again and again.
    • Deliberation, as in a civic assembly, asks residents to weigh tradeoffs together. Every method has its own strengths and limits.

    Feedback

    Members of the public get a chance to weigh in on policies before they are final.

    Why it matters. Feedback is a chance for officials to explain what they are considering and why. It also lets the public offer data, personal experience, professional expertise, preferences, and community needs. Utah book

    What it looks like. Public comment periods, hearings, surveys, listening sessions, and advisory councils. Local examples:

    • Who is heard. Good feedback reflects the whole community, not just a few loud voices. But comment processes often favor the most motivated people, professionals, and advocacy groups with their own goals. Utah book Doing this well takes funding and training many departments don’t have.

    Deliberation

    Sometimes the public is invited to discuss and weigh the tradeoffs of a decision together.

    Why it matters. Inside government, deliberation happens all the time: councils and boards debate before they vote. The public is rarely asked to do this, but when it is done well, it can produce well-informed policies that the public supports. Wendy Willis put it this way: when people must decide together, “the incentives are problem-solving incentives rather than rhetorical incentives.” Taha Elwefati added that meeting face to face keeps an issue from becoming something flat to yell at online. CC Mtg 1, Sep 25

    What it looks like. Juries, advisory bodies, task forces, and civic assemblies, where a randomly selected group of residents learns about an issue and makes recommendations. Examples:

    • Deschutes County formed a civic assembly on youth homelessness in fall 2025. Added Bend City Manager’s Report, Nov 2025
    • Oregon’s Citizens’ Initiative Review brings together a random panel of voters to study a ballot measure and write a statement for the voters’ pamphlet. Added verify current status
    • Mosier, Oregon. After a train derailment, Oregon’s Kitchen Table ran a quick community conversation so the town knew what residents wanted before negotiating with the railroad. Mtg 2 team debrief, internal
    • Cambridge, Massachusetts convened a civic assembly within months of a dispute over gunshot-detection microphones. Mtg 2 team debrief, internal Verify

    After a decision: accountability and appeal

    Key points

    • Accountability means reporting results, justifying spending, and closing the loop with the people who gave input.
    • Oregon expects to publish a list of state AI uses, and Prineville’s taxing districts are choosing how to spend new data center tax revenue.
    • Ways to challenge a decision include school board appeals, the Land Use Board of Appeals, public meeting grievances, and ballot measures.
    • In land use appeals, an issue usually must be raised at the local hearing first (“raise it or waive it”).

    Accountability

    Government shares what happened after a policy takes effect, checks that it was a good use of public money, and is ready to change course.

    Why it matters. Accountability means public officials, through open processes, explain their decisions, justify how public money was used, report on results, and take responsibility for how things turned out. Utah book It also means closing the loop with the people who gave input. Kathi Barguil said institutions often lose people at this step: they ask for ideas, then never say how the input was used or why it wasn’t. “Community members want to know that the time that they put in mattered.” CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14, ~57:23

    What it looks like.

    • State of Oregon. A public list of state AI uses is expected within about six months, along with an annual report to the Governor and an AI adoption plan from each agency. Public-facing AI must have a way for users to give feedback, with review and escalation. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9
    • Prineville. The first data center property becomes taxable in 2027. The local taxing districts are jointly choosing projects each has heard about from the community. What role the public has in that choice is an open question delegates could consider. CC Mtg 2, Oct 1; CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14
    • Audits. The Oregon Secretary of State’s Audits Division reviews how state agencies spend money and whether programs work, and publishes its findings. Added add link

    Appeal

    If a decision or policy isn’t working, or wasn’t made properly, the public should have ways to challenge it.

    What it looks like.

    • School decisions. In Bend-La Pine, a concern goes first to the school, then to the superintendent’s office, then to the school board, which decides whether an action followed policy. Karen Rush mtg, Oct 15
    • Land use decisions can be appealed to the Land Use Board of Appeals (LUBA). One catch: an issue usually must be raised at the local hearing, or it can’t be raised on appeal (“raise it or waive it”). EDCO written submission, Oct 13 Added ORS 197.763
    • Public meeting violations. Anyone can file a written grievance with the public body within 30 days; the body has 21 days to respond. If it doesn’t fix the problem, the person can complain to the Oregon Government Ethics Commission. Courts can also void decisions made in an unlawful meeting. Added City of Springfield summary of OGEC training
    • Pushing for proper process. Oregon Housing and Community Services emailed a handbook change barring funded organizations from using AI, “effective immediately.” NeighborImpact argued that enforceable guidance must go through rulemaking with public comment, and the change is now on hold. CC Mtg 3, Oct 9
    • Ballot measures. Oregon voters can refer laws and many local decisions to the ballot, or propose their own through an initiative. Added verify local referral rules

    What laws shape public engagement? The floor

    Key points

    • In Oregon, the public has a right to watch most government decisions being made, but usually not a right to speak, except in cases like land use and budget hearings.
    • The law guarantees openings in public meetings, land use, budgets, state rulemaking, utilities, and tax incentive decisions.
    • How much residents can change a land use project depends on timing: the most when the rules are set, very little once a project is allowed “by right.”
    • Executive offices and staff can make many decisions with little public review.

    The ceiling: where could the public engage?

    Key points

    • The “ceiling” is how much power the public could have, beyond what the law requires.
    • Arnstein’s ladder (1969) runs from manipulation at the bottom to citizen control at the top, and treats higher rungs as better.
    • The newer IAP2 Spectrum treats each level as fitting some decisions but not others.
    • Models raised by the team include participatory budgeting, community benefit agreements, and civic assemblies.

    The “ceiling” is how much power the public could have, beyond what the law requires. One well-known way to picture this is Sherry Arnstein’s “Ladder of Citizen Participation” (1969). Each rung up gives residents more real say over the outcome. Added Arnstein, S. (1969), “A Ladder of Citizen Participation,” Journal of the American Institute of Planners 35(4)

    Arnstein’s ladder is a critique, and it treats higher rungs as better. A newer, more neutral tool is the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation (Inform, Consult, Involve, Collaborate, Empower), which treats each level as fitting some decisions but not others. Whether every decision should reach the top is a question for delegates. Added IAP2 Spectrum; add link

    What are we talking about when we say “AI”?

    Key points

    • AI is a broad term for computer systems that can perform tasks with human-like intelligence, like understanding language or recognizing images.
    • Most AI tools run on models: giant sets of numbers that capture patterns in training data, built by a handful of large labs and companies.
    • AI isn’t new, but today’s large language models are far more flexible. ChatGPT’s release in late 2022 brought them to the public.
    • Companies are now building AI agents that take actions on their own. Experts disagree about how far and how fast this will go.

    In short, artificial intelligence (AI) is “a broad term for computer systems that can perform tasks with human-like intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing images, learning from data, reasoning, and making decisions” (Stanford HAI).

    Unlike traditional software, which follows rules written step by step by programmers, modern AI systems “learn” from data. They find patterns across millions or billions of examples and use those patterns to make predictions or responses in new situations (adapted from the Utah Briefing Book).

    The core technology: models

    Most of today’s AI tools run on models. A model is essentially just very complex statistics. It is a giant set of numbers that captures the patterns found in the data it was trained on.

    • Who builds them: Models are developed by AI research labs and large corporations, such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and Microsoft.
    • What they need: Building (or “training”) a model requires a massive amount of data, along with huge amounts of computing power and electricity.
    • How we use them: Most people never touch a model directly. Instead, they use an app (like ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, or a school tool like MagicSchool) that sends their request to a model running in a data center somewhere else.

    How is this different from what already existed?

    Before: AI was already being used for all sorts of things long before ChatGPT. Recommendation algorithms on social media, search engines, spam filters, GPS directions, and voice assistants like Siri and Alexa all use AI. Most people used it every day without thinking of it as “AI.”

    Now: Today’s models are significantly more flexible and capable than before. Large language models (LLMs) can turn plain-English requests (“prompts”) into all sorts of other outputs: written documents, computer code, images, spreadsheets, summaries, and translations. The turning point for public awareness came in late 2022 with the release of ChatGPT. For the first time, millions of people could talk directly with an AI system that could write, answer questions, and create (adapted from the Utah Briefing Book).

    What could it do?

    AI companies are now building “AI agents.” These are systems that don’t just answer questions but take actions on their own, like booking appointments, filling out forms, or writing and running computer code. Some researchers and companies believe AI may eventually match or exceed human abilities across most tasks. Others are skeptical. Experts disagree significantly about when, or whether, this might happen (Stanford HAI, 2025, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book).

    Key points

    • AI depends on a physical system: internet connections, specialized chips, data centers, and electricity.
    • Data centers store data, run everyday apps, and, newest of all, train AI models.

    • Data centers used about 23% of Oregon’s retail electricity sales in 2025, and information on their water use is often incomplete.

    What do data centers do?

    Data centers are not new. They have been the backbone of the internet for decades (adapted from the Utah Briefing Book). They do three main jobs:

    1. Store and retrieve data (“cloud computing”). This is the most familiar use of data centers, and what the current AI industry is built on. Even before the AI industry took off, data centers were needed to store the data for websites, stream videos on sites like YouTube, and back up photos and email. Over the past few decades, more and more of our information has been stored in data centers.
    2. Run calculations for everyday apps (“inference”). Data centers have always done the computing behind everyday apps. When you search for something on Google, the algorithms that power the search run on a computer in a data center. Each time someone asks an AI chatbot a question, a data center does that work too.
    3. Train AI models. This is the newest use for data centers. Training a large model can take thousands of specialized chips running nonstop for weeks or months.

    Demand for data centers has increased significantly as AI has taken off. The hardware has changed too. A regular rack of computer servers draws 5 to 10 kilowatts of power, while a rack built for AI draws 40 to 80 kilowatts or more (Pew Research Center, citing IEA, 2024, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book).

    Some nuance: individual vs. local vs. corporate use

    It helps to separate three different scales of AI energy use:

    • Individual: A single AI prompt uses about as much electricity as a 10-watt LED bulb running for 90 seconds. One person’s daily use is small (Digit.in, 2025, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book).
    • Local: The impact on a community comes from the data center itself. A single large facility can use as much electricity as tens of thousands of homes, all in one place. That is why data centers have become local questions about land, water, power lines, and permits.
    • Corporate: Most of the demand comes from AI companies and cloud providers (like Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta), which use electricity both to build AI models and to run them for millions of users at once (adapted from the Utah Briefing Book).

    Power: how much electricity?

    • Nationally: U.S. data centers used about 4.4% of the nation’s electricity in 2023 (Pew Research Center, citing IEA, 2024, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book).
    • In Oregon: Data centers used about 23% of the state’s retail electricity sales in 2025. That share is expected to grow to 31 to 32% by 2030, or about 25 terawatt-hours a year, enough to power about 2.5 million homes (OPB). Oregon’s data centers already use electricity equal to about 71% of all the electricity sold to Oregon homes (KPTV). New source
    • In Prineville: Meta and Apple have paid for public infrastructure, including an underground water storage system and solar farms in the high desert to help meet their energy needs (Marketplace).

    Why is AI relevant now?

    Key points

    • Communities nationwide are pushing back on data centers, and a 2026 poll found two-thirds of Oregon voters support a temporary halt on new AI data centers.
    • In October 2026, Gov. Kotek paused new pollution permits for data centers until July 2027, and several Oregon cities have passed their own temporary bans.
    • AI investment is enormous, but economists disagree about how much it’s actually driving growth.

    Economically

    AI spending is propping up the economy, but economists disagree on how much. Harvard economist Jason Furman estimated that investment in data centers and information-processing technology made up about 92% of U.S. economic growth in the first half of 2025 (Fortune). Goldman Sachs economists disagree. They argue that because so many chips and parts are imported, AI’s real boost to U.S. growth was “basically zero” (Gizmodo). New source (both)

    There is a lot of promise, but also a lot of speculation. Major economic institutions project that AI could raise productivity if widely adopted (IMF, 2024; McKinsey Global Institute, 2023; Goldman Sachs, 2023, as cited in the Utah Briefing Book). At the same time, the amount of money being spent is enormous: one estimate puts AI investment in data centers, power, networks, and chips at $10.3 trillion from 2025 to 2032 (Brookings). If the expected returns don’t show up, communities could be left with half-used buildings and power lines that someone still has to pay for.

    Who gets the benefits, and who carries the risks? The answer is not always the same group of people. For example:

    • Taxes: Without tax breaks, Meta’s and Apple’s Prineville data centers would have owed about $31 million in property taxes last year. With them, the two companies paid about $347,000 combined (Marketplace). Statewide, data centers paid $60.2 million in property taxes in 2025, while exemptions were worth an estimated $230 million to $450 million (OPB).
    • Jobs: Data centers create many temporary construction jobs, but relatively few permanent ones. Of Oregon’s 2,630 direct data center workers, most live in Eastern Oregon (OPB).
    • Power bills: When utilities build new power plants and lines for data centers, someone has to pay. Oregon’s POWER Act (2025) is meant to keep those costs from shifting onto regular households (Central Oregon Decisions tracker).

    What more investment and integration could mean: As AI is built into more products and services, it gets harder for any one community to say “no” to it. Local governments may feel they need to keep up so they don’t get “left behind” (see What makes AI-related decisions different).

    2. Permissions and planning

    In the local context, this is most relevant to data centers and other physical AI investments. The goal of these decisions is to figure out whether, where, and on what terms a community wants new development.

    Planning sets the long-term vision for a community’s economy and land.

    • Comprehensive Economic Development Strategy (CEDS): A regional economic plan developed by the Central Oregon Intergovernmental Council (COIC).
    • Economic Opportunities Analysis (EOA): Oregon’s land use rules require cities to plan for enough land and economic opportunities to support their expected growth over the next 20 years.     What makes AI-related decisions different from other government decisions?

    Key points

    • In many ways, AI decisions resemble decisions about other major industries, like energy.
    • Speed: technology moves faster than the rules, and the scale of investment is larger than past booms like railroads and highways.
    • Private development: AI systems are hard to see inside, and well-funded companies can engage government in ways residents can’t.
    • Loss of agency: AI often automates human judgment, and many people feel they have little say.

    In some ways, AI-related decisions are not very different from other decisions government agencies make. It is generally helpful to compare AI to other major industries, like energy (oil, gas, hydropower, renewables). Like a new power plant or factory, a data center brings jobs and tax revenue but also uses land, water, and electricity. Local officials have pointed out that Prineville’s existing data centers are not AI data centers, and that many of the same land use and tax tools apply CC Mtg 4 Pt 1, Oct 14.     However, it’s worth calling out a few things that make this industry different.

    Speed: technology moves faster than the rules

    The speed of technological progress outpaces our ability to regulate it. Researchers call this the Collingridge dilemma, named after a 1980 book on controlling technology. It describes a double bind: “Early on, intervention is possible but impacts are uncertain. Once impacts become clear, the technology is entrenched” (Wikipedia, summarizing The Social Control of Technology). This is closely related to the “pacing problem,” the idea that new technology increasingly outpaces laws and regulations.

    Content committee member Rachel Haakenson put it this way: regulation tends to follow adoption, which creates the feeling of a race CC Mtg 1, Sep 25.

    The speed and size of investment is larger than anything we’ve seen before. One Brookings analysis projects that investment in AI data center buildings, power systems, networks, and specialized chips will total $10.3 trillion from 2025 to 2032. That’s an average of 3.63% of U.S. gross domestic product (GDP) every year. “The projected buildout would be larger relative to the economy than the major U.S. canal, railroad, electrification, highway, and telecommunications investment booms,” writes the author, Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh of Columbia University (Brookings).

    Private development: public input can be limited or outcompeted

    The “black box” challenge. It is hard to know exactly how AI systems work, what data they were (or were not) trained on, and why they give the answers they do.

    • Many layers between the community and the model. Most AI tools are provided through private service providers, who themselves depend on AI models built by a few very large companies (like Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft). This makes it difficult to change how the models behave to meet the specific needs or concerns of local residents.
    • It doesn’t have to be this way. Communities and governments can also use locally governed models or “open-weight” models, whose inner workings can be inspected and adjusted.
    • Some things can’t be taken back. Content committee member Marcio Nunes noted that institutional knowledge fed into shared AI systems can’t be “un-taught,” which raises questions about ownership and trust CC Mtg 1, Sep 25.

    Well-funded companies can engage governments in ways regular residents can’t. High investment in AI companies often means they have the time, budget, and skill to work with city and regional governments far more than everyday residents can. Companies like Waymo and Flock have invested heavily in public relations and government outreach. Playbooks for winning local approval are being developed in a way that feels notably different from earlier industrial revolutions.     • The way AI systems are deployed often means automating human judgment: decisions once made by a person (a teacher, a clerk, a police officer) are now shaped or made by software.

    Case study: Loudoun County shifts away from by-right approval of data centers

    Key points

    • Whether the public gets a say on a project at all is often decided by obscure zoning rules. Changing the approval pathway, from staff approval to public hearings and a board vote, was the lever that guaranteed residents a voice on every new project.
    • Timing matters. By the time the rules changed, roughly 250 data centers had been built and dozens more were already in the pipeline. Those pending applications were grandfathered in, and a 2026 attempt to undo that stalled over the threat of developer lawsuits.
    • The county had to weigh a real tradeoff in public: data centers pay for a large share of the county budget and have lowered property tax rates, but many residents decided the noise, power lines, and sprawl weren’t worth it.

    After two decades of courting the industry, the Virginia county with the world’s largest concentration of data centers changed its zoning rules so that every new data center must go through public hearings and a vote by elected officials, rather than a staff-level sign-off.

    Key Sources: Loudoun Now on the March 2025 vote, Holland & Knight summary, County statement on the 2026 pause, Bisnow on the grandfathering fight, County initiative page

    Basic information

    Loudoun County sits in Northern Virginia, about an hour outside Washington, D.C. Starting when data centers were still a niche industry, the county deliberately recruited them, and the area around Ashburn became known as “Data Center Alley,” which the county describes as the largest concentration of data centers in the world. The payoff was enormous: the local Chamber of Commerce reported that data centers generated about $1.2 billion, or 38% of all county revenue, in fiscal year 2026, and the real property tax rate fell from the highest in Virginia to about 80 cents per $100 over fifteen years.

    For years, data centers were a “by-right” use in many of the county’s industrial zones. That meant that if a proposal met the written standards in the zoning ordinance, county staff approved it administratively, with no public hearing and no vote by elected officials. In other areas, data centers needed a “special exception,” which requires staff review, a public hearing and recommendation from the appointed Planning Commission, and a second public hearing and final vote by the elected Board of Supervisors.

    As the buildings multiplied and crept closer to neighborhoods, so did the complaints: constant equipment noise, new high-voltage transmission lines and substations, thousands of diesel backup generators, and strain on the regional power grid. Board Chair Phyllis Randall said data centers had become the most frequent complaint she heard. Residents who once accepted the argument about lower taxes were now telling her, “I will pay more. Stop building these.” In 2021, Supervisor Juli Briskman proposed remapping where data centers should be allowed, which grew into the county’s two-phase “Data Center Standards and Locations” initiative.

    What did it change? What was interesting about it?

    • Every decision moved onto the public record. In March 2025, the Board voted 7–2 to make data centers a special exception use everywhere in the county. This didn’t ban data centers; it changed who decides and where. Every new application now goes through two public hearings and a vote by elected officials.
    • The appointed advisory body and the elected board disagreed. The Planning Commission narrowly recommended against the change in late 2024, preferring more targeted fixes such as steering data centers toward transmission corridors. The Board went ahead anyway.
    • Grandfathering was the compromise. In a separate 5–4 vote, the Board let applications filed before its February 2025 public hearing continue under the old rules, as long as they were more than 500 feet from homes. That covered 22 applications. Industry groups and the Chamber supported it; environmental groups and many residents opposed it, and Supervisor Briskman argued it amounted to approving 22 new data centers.
    • Process first, standards second. Phase 1 settled who decides. Phase 2, underway since September 2025 and expected to finish in 2027, is working out what the rules should be: siting near homes, substations, on-site power generation and battery storage, noise, and building heights.
    • A pause while the rules are written. In September 2026, the Board voted 7–1–1 to pause consideration of new data center and substation applications for up to 12 months while Phase 2 is completed. The county attorney advised that an outright moratorium would be illegal under Virginia law, so the pause was structured around the state’s 12-month limit for acting on applications. A formal resolution is scheduled for the Board’s November 17, 2026 meeting.

    What worked

    • A structural guarantee instead of case-by-case fights. Rather than residents having to discover and organize against each project, the rule change made public hearings automatic for every new data center.
    • Sustained public pressure changed the Board’s stance. Over a few years, the Board went from the industry’s chief recruiter to its regulator. Advocacy groups like the Piedmont Environmental Council kept the issue visible through every hearing, and the Chair explicitly cited residents’ voices as the reason for the change.
    • Splitting the problem in two. Settling the process question first let the county act relatively quickly, while the slower, more technical work on standards continues with its own round of public input.

    What didn’t

    • The fix came late. When by-right approval ended, the county already had about 46 million square feet of data centers built or permitted, and much of the remaining land was already zoned for more. Public input now mostly shapes the margins.
    • Earlier decisions proved hard to undo. In October 2026, a proposal to revoke the grandfathered status of the remaining legacy projects (about 10.5 million square feet) was tabled for lack of support. County staff warned it could expose the county to $60 million or more in lawsuits and damage its reputation with businesses, and even some supervisors who had opposed grandfathering argued it was unfair to change the rules after developers relied on them.
    • Project-by-project hearings can’t answer the big question. A special exception hearing is still a yes-or-no vote on one project at a time. The larger questions, like how much data center growth the county wants and what it’s willing to give up in revenue to limit it, are being worked out separately and slowly.
    • The county depends on what it’s trying to rein in. With more than a third of county revenue coming from data centers, every new restriction carries real budget consequences, a point the business community raised throughout the debate.

    Case study: The Hanford Advisory Board on nuclear waste

    Key points

    • The Board’s formal recommendations were rarely followed by the departments that received them. Instead, early, informal input in the relevant committee meetings is what helped.
    • The HAB was able to gather and present credible, independent information in front of the media and through their civil society connections, which often helped to sway decisions in the right direction.
    • Long terms and real interest gave the delegates enough technical fluency to hold experts to account.

    After four decades of plutonium production tainted the Columbia River watershed, a 37-person board representing different sectors of Washington and Oregon was assembled to present consensus-based recommendations to federal departments on how to clean it up.

    Key Sources: Case Study

    Basic information

    From 1944 to 1989, the Hanford Site near Richland, Washington, on the Columbia River, secretly produced most of the plutonium for the U.S. nuclear arsenal. Documents released in 1986 revealed leaking waste tanks, contaminated water dumped into the river and soil, and deliberate radiation releases. In 1989, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), the EPA, and Washington’s Department of Ecology signed the Tri-Party Agreement to clean up the site under federal laws that require public participation. When a 1991 federal report named public distrust of DOE as a major obstacle to the cleanup and recommended advisory boards, the HAB followed, formally launching in 1994.

    DOE first convened a working group of regional stakeholders on future uses of the site, many of whom went on to join the HAB. The Tri-Party Agencies then commissioned the Keystone Center, a nonprofit conflict-resolution group, to interview stakeholders and draft the Board’s design in 1993. The founding members secured commitments that the Board could set its own agenda, control its own budget and staff, and get answers from senior agency leadership. The Board was meant to include every group with a “clearly definable stake” in the cleanup: local governments, the Hanford workforce, tribal nations, the state of Oregon, regional environmental and public-interest groups, public health, universities, local business, and members of the public at large, alongside the agencies themselves. Each constituency nominates its own representatives, who are expected to consult with it regularly.

    What did it change? What was interesting about it?

    The HAB put lay citizens inside highly technical decisions — risk thresholds, cleanup technologies, a 6,000-page tank-waste environmental impact statement — a domain usually left to experts. Several design choices made that possible:

    • Representation by constituency, not by lottery or open door. Members answer to the groups that sent them. That gave the Board credibility with DOE: a strong negative reaction from the HAB signaled a likely public backlash, which one regulator likened to a “canary in the coal mine.”
    • Long tenure builds expertise. Members said it takes two to four years to become fluent in agency and engineering vocabulary. With no term limits, some members had more institutional memory than the regulators they advised.
    • Graded consensus. Members could fully agree, “live with” a recommendation, or register reservations without blocking it. This pushed members across the Board’s East–West divide (pro-nuclear Tri-Cities locals vs. Portland and Seattle environmental groups) to find common ground, and made the resulting advice harder for DOE to dismiss as activism.
    • Self-direction. The Board sets its own agenda, controls its own budget and some staff, and works through five standing committees.
    • A multi-agency table. EPA and Washington Ecology sit alongside DOE, exposing members to competing agency views and making it harder for any one agency to capture the Board.

    Concrete changes include stronger protections against beryllium exposure for Hanford workers (see below), a Groundwater Values document and decision flowchart that the agencies adopted, and extended public comment periods. The authors’ broader claim is that the HAB acts as a check on “bureaucratic domination”: it narrows the information gap between agencies and the public they affect.

    What worked

    • Early, informal input shaped DOE’s plans more than formal advice did. DOE brought draft proposals to committee meetings, and when members pushed back hard, DOE often adjusted course before its plans were fixed.
    • Seats tied to real constituencies gave the Board credibility. Because members answer to local governments, unions, tribes, and advocacy groups, DOE treated their reactions as a preview of public response. One regulator called the Board a “canary in the coal mine.”
    • Public, consensus-backed advice became leverage when DOE stalled. In 2009 the Board warned that beryllium exposure was a bigger hazard to workers than radiation. DOE’s first reply was defensive, but after the local paper built coverage on the Board’s advice, DOE reversed course in late 2010 and implemented most of its recommendations.
    • Long terms let lay members hold experts to account. Members said fluency in the technical language takes two to four years, and with no term limits, some outlasted the regulators they advised.

    What didn’t

    • Formal recommendations were rarely adopted. The Board issued 258 advice letters by 2012, but in 2011 DOE committed to change on none of 93 recommendations and gave no response to 20 of them.
    • The broader public stayed on the sidelines. Visitors sit in a gallery behind the table, speak only in brief comment periods, and rarely attend.
    • The Board hesitated to take its criticism public. Many members’ communities depend on Hanford jobs, and open conflict risks the trust that informal influence relies on. Even the beryllium win took more than a year and the persistence of a single member.

    Case study: Oakland’s Privacy Commission on surveillance technologies

    Key points

    • The Commission’s power comes from timing. Under a 2018 ordinance, any city department that wants a surveillance technology must bring it to the Commission, with a written impact report and use policy, before acquiring it, and report on its use every year after.
    • Notable wins include a warrant requirement for police cell-phone tracking devices and the end of a little-known agreement between the police and federal immigration enforcement.
    • Still, the Council doesn’t always listen to the Privacy Commission’s recommendations, and a volunteer board with no staff of its own struggles to get complete information from the departments it oversees.

    When residents learned the city planned to turn a port security system into a citywide surveillance hub, a coalition of residents, community groups, and privacy advocates pushed the city to scale it back, and won a standing citizen commission that reviews every surveillance technology the city wants to use before the City Council decides.

    Key Sources: Policing Project, Enabling legislation, Oaklandside on the Commission’s limits (2021), KQED on the 2025 Flock vote, ACLU of Northern California on the Commission’s origins

    Basic information

    In July 2013, Oakland accepted a federal Homeland Security grant to build a “Domain Awareness Center” (DAC). Originally meant to monitor the Port of Oakland, the plan grew into a citywide hub that would pull together feeds from roughly 700 cameras (including at schools and public housing), license plate readers, gunshot detectors, and facial recognition software. This came just as Edward Snowden’s leaks about federal surveillance were making headlines.

    A coalition of residents, community groups like Oakland Privacy, and the ACLU of Northern California pushed back through lobbying, packed council meetings, and street protests. Brian Hofer, who had never set foot in City Hall before, became one of the leading voices. In March 2014, the City Council scaled the DAC back to the port and airport and created an ad hoc citizens’ committee, with members appointed by each councilmember, to write a privacy and data retention policy before the scaled-back center could switch on. It took about a year, partly because there were so few models to borrow from anywhere in the country.

    The ad hoc committee recommended making itself permanent. In January 2016, the Council created the nine-member Privacy Advisory Commission (PAC), which began meeting that July. The Commission’s first major task was drafting a citywide surveillance law with city staff, which the Council adopted in May 2018.

    What did it change? What was interesting about it?

    • Review happens up front, not after the fact. Any city department that wants to acquire surveillance technology must first go before the Commission with a Surveillance Impact Report and a proposed Use Policy, even if the technology costs nothing. The Commission recommends; the Council decides.
    • Review continues after approval. Departments must submit annual reports on how they use existing surveillance tools, so the Commission can keep weighing costs and benefits over time rather than giving a one-time yes or no.
    • Citizens wrote the rules themselves. The ad hoc committee wrote the DAC’s privacy policy, and the permanent Commission drafted the surveillance ordinance that defines its own role.
    • Enforcement through the courts. The ordinance lets any resident sue the city for violating it, which gave the Commission’s work teeth that its advisory status alone does not.
    • Not anti-technology by default. The Commission has backed some tools, unanimously supporting continued use of ShotSpotter gunshot detection and, in 2023, recommending a policy for about 300 new license plate reader cameras. Its job is to weigh tradeoffs, not just say no.

    What worked

    • Surfacing information that would otherwise stay internal. At the Commission’s second meeting in 2016, a police deputy chief publicly explained for the first time how the department used cell-site simulators, devices that mimic cell towers to locate phones, which it had been using since 2006. The Commission drafted a policy requiring warrants and yearly reports, and the Council adopted it.
    • Investigations led to real policy changes. The Commission’s look into police assistance during a 2017 ICE raid in West Oakland uncovered an agreement with ICE that the Council later terminated.
    • A concrete fight built a lasting constituency. Because the Commission grew out of the DAC campaign, it had organized community support from day one, and groups like Oakland Privacy kept showing up to watchdog its work. It became a national model that other cities have studied.

    What didn’t

    • Advisory only, and the Council can override it. In 2021, after police failed to produce required audits and admitted keeping license plate data for two years instead of the six months its policy allowed, the Commission recommended suspending the program. A Council committee instead just asked the department to hand over the documents. In 2025, the Commission voted 4–2 against renewing the city’s contract with Flock Safety over concerns about license plate data being shared with federal agencies. A Council committee deadlocked on it, but the full Council approved the contract 7–1 in December 2025 with added privacy amendments. Two commissioners had resigned the month before, frustrated that their warnings kept being overridden.
    • It depends on departments sharing information. The police department repeatedly failed to hand over records the Commission requested. Its chair was told to file public records requests like any member of the public, and eventually sued the city in 2021 as a private citizen to get them.
    • Volunteers with no staff burn out. Commissioners have day jobs and no attorney, investigator, or dedicated staff, so they mostly rely on what city departments tell them. Members described fatigue and difficulty trusting they had the full picture, and Hofer later concluded the model can’t work without an inspector general to dig into the data on the commission’s behalf.
    • Heavy reliance on one champion. Commissioners worried openly about what would happen when Hofer left. He stepped down in 2025, around the same time the Council overrode the Commission on the Flock contract.
